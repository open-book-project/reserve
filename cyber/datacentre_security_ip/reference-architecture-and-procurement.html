    Chapter 15: Reference Architecture and Procurement¶

    The earlier chapters described the parts. This last chapter condenses them into an eight-layer defence-in-depth reference architecture for an AI data centre, with minimum and advanced requirements for each layer, a checklist of procurement questions to put to vendors, and a timeline that shows how recently most of this has arrived.

    15.1 Eight layers, built from the bottom up¶

    Figure 15.1 stacks eight layers. From the bottom: 1 Silicon (integrated RoT, fuses and PUF, TRNG, crypto and PQC engines, memory encryption, sensors, lifecycle); 2 Device firmware (secure and measured boot, signed and anti-rollback updates, PQC dual signing, OCP recovery); 3 Platform (PRoT with firmware resilience, hardened BMC on DC-SCM, TPM, platform certificates, SPDM fleet attestation); 4 Fabric and interconnect (PCIe and CXL IDE with TDISP, NVLink and UALink encryption, MACsec, IPsec and PSP, isolated out-of-band network); 5 Host and virtualisation (confidential VMs, hardened hypervisor, I/O offload, vTPM); 6 AI workload (encrypted weights and checkpoints, attestation-gated key release, confidential containers, model-registry signing); 7 Security operations (SIEM and XDR, firmware inventory and drift, insider controls, two-person rule, egress limits on weights); 8 Facility and OT (physical zones, IEC 62443 OT segmentation, secure UPS and PDU cards). Across all of them runs governance: NIST 800-193, 800-53 and CSF 2.0, ISO 27001, SOC 2, IEC 62443, FIPS 140-3, CNSA 2.0, the EU CRA and NIS2, and OCP S.A.F.E. The principle is stated at the foot of the figure: trust is built bottom-up, and each layer is only as trustworthy as the measured, attested layer beneath it.

    15.2 Minimum and advanced requirements¶

    Layer Minimum (SL2 to SL3) Advanced (SL4 to SL5, frontier AI)
    Silicon Integrated RoT with secure boot in CPU, GPU, NIC, SSD and BMC; debug disabled in production Caliptra or OpenTitan-class open RoTs; PQC (ML-DSA, LMS) verification keys; anti-tamper sensors; vendor S.A.F.E. reports
    Device firmware Signed updates with anti-rollback; vendor SBOMs Dual classical and PQC signatures; reproducible builds; owner-controlled signing (ownership transfer)
    Platform NIST 800-193 PRoT or OEM silicon RoT; TPM 2.0 measured boot; BMC never on production networks DC-SCM with a PRoT aggregating SPDM evidence from every device; platform certificates verified at receiving
    Fabric Isolated out-of-band network; MACsec or IPsec on inter-site links; TLS everywhere PCIe and CXL IDE with TDISP; NVLink or UALink encryption; PSP or IPsec on RDMA; per-tenant key domains
    Host Hardened hypervisor; vTPM per VM Confidential VMs with GPU CC; attestation-gated workload admission
    AI workload Encrypted weights and checkpoints at rest; RBAC on the model registry Weights decrypted only inside attested TEEs; HSM-wrapped keys; signed model artefacts; egress bandwidth caps
    Operations SIEM, vulnerability management, privileged-access management Two-person rule for weight access; continuous fleet attestation; insider-risk programme; red teaming
    Facility TIA-942 or EN 50600 zones; segmented BMS and OT Mantraps and biometrics for AI halls; DIMM and bus-tamper deterrence; OT monitoring; vetted staff and escorts

    15.3 Procurement questions to ask vendors¶

    • Which RoT (vendor, product, version) is in each chip and board, and is its ROM or RTL open or third-party audited (an OCP S.A.F.E. report)?
    • Does every device support SPDM 1.2 or later attestation with a certificate chain to a vendor root, and are reference measurements (RIM and CoRIM) published?
    • What happens on firmware corruption: is there automatic recovery (NIST 800-193 and OCP Recovery) without a technician?
    • Are firmware-signing keys held in FIPS 140-3 Level 3 HSMs, and is there a PQC signing roadmap (LMS or ML-DSA) consistent with CNSA 2.0?
    • For GPUs, NICs and SSDs: are CC, TEE-I/O, IDE and TDISP supported in the shipped firmware, and what is the performance cost?
    • Can the owner take ownership of the RoT (owner keys, ownership transfer), and how is the device sanitised at end of life?
    • Which facility controllers (CDU, PDU, UPS, BMS) carry IEC 62443-4-2 or UL 2900 certification, and how are their updates signed?

    15.4 A short history¶

    Figure 15.2 is a timeline from 2009 to 2030. It shows how recently the mechanisms of this book arrived: TPM 1.2 and TPM 2.0 (2009 to 2015); Google Titan, Microsoft Cerberus and AWS Nitro (2017); NIST SP 800-193 (2018); OpenTitan and SPDM 1.0 (2019); AMD SEV-SNP, DC-SCM and the TDX specification (2020 to 2021); Caliptra, H100 confidential computing, PCIe 6.0 IDE and TDISP (2022); Intel TDX general availability and OCP S.A.F.E. (2023); the post-quantum FIPS, Blackwell TEE-I/O and Azure Integrated HSM (2024); Caliptra 2.0 and 2.1 with PQC, Lattice MachXO5-NX TDQ, UALink 1.0 and the 2025 interposer attacks (2025); Vera Rubin NVL72 rack-scale confidential computing, AMD Helios, OpenTitan in production and the Microchip TS1800 (2026); and after that the EU Cyber Resilience Act, fully applicable in December 2027, CNSA 2.0 exclusive post-quantum for firmware signing and networking by 2030, and NIST IR 8547 deprecation of RSA and ECC in 2030 and disallowance in 2035.

    15.5 Summary¶

    • The reference architecture has eight layers: silicon, device firmware, platform, fabric, host, AI workload, security operations and facility, under a governance layer of standards.
    • Trust is built bottom-up: each layer is only as trustworthy as the measured, attested layer beneath it.
    • Minimum requirements (SL2 to SL3) ensure RoTs, signed updates, isolated management and encryption; advanced requirements (SL4 to SL5) add open RoTs, PQC, TDISP and IDE, attested TEEs, HSM-wrapped weight keys, two-person control and continuous attestation.
    • Seven procurement questions turn the architecture into a vendor checklist.

    15.6 Exercises¶

    1. Why is "bottom-up" the right way to build the reference architecture?

      Answer

      Each layer relies on measurements and attestation from the layer beneath it. If the silicon and firmware are not trustworthy, nothing above can be, however good the software is.

    2. Name two differences between the minimum and advanced requirements for the platform layer.

      Answer

      Minimum: a NIST 800-193 PRoT or OEM silicon RoT with TPM measured boot, and a BMC never on production networks. Advanced: a DC-SCM whose PRoT aggregates SPDM evidence from every device, and platform certificates verified at receiving.

    3. A vendor says its servers are "secure by design". Write three questions from the checklist to test the claim.

      Answer

      For example: Which RoT is in each chip and is it open or audited (S.A.F.E.)? Does every device support SPDM attestation with published reference measurements? What happens on firmware corruption, and is recovery automatic?

    4. Which regulation becomes fully applicable in December 2027, and what do CNSA 2.0 and NIST IR 8547 require by 2030?

      Answer

      The EU Cyber Resilience Act. CNSA 2.0 requires exclusive post-quantum algorithms for firmware signing and networking equipment by 2030, and NIST IR 8547 (draft) proposes deprecating RSA and ECC by 2030.
