    Chapter 10: Additional Tests (I): Bias, Entropy, Correlation and Compression¶

    The next five chapters describe twenty-one further tests, grouped by what they look for. They come from a reference implementation written by the author, and they are included for two reasons: each is a compact lesson in how to design a test with an exact or well-understood null distribution, and the suite as a whole shows how to calibrate tests on known-good data and combine their results into an honest verdict. This first chapter covers the core statistical tests: bias, multi-scale entropy, correlation, entropy stability across windows, periodicity and predictability, the Shannon entropy test and compression.

    10.1 The additional tests and how to read their results¶

    The tests of Chapters 10 to 14 are 21 numbered tests: 18 statistical tests that each give a p-value, 2 generator tests that either find a matching rule or do not, and test 8, which reads all the others and gives a verdict. They come from a reference implementation written by the author, and they were built so that every result can be explained and defended. In these chapters "we" and "our" mean that implementation and its measurements.

    Group Tests What the group asks
    Core statistical tests 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 9, 10 Is the data even, independent and incompressible?
    TRNG/QRNG source tests 11, 12, 13 Does it behave like raw physical noise, or stall?
    Advanced mathematical tests U1, U2, U3, U4, U5, U6 Is there structure that needs heavier mathematics to see?
    Research-grade tests H1, H2 Can a trained predictor win? Do Hamming weights depend on each other?
    Generator proofs 6, 7 Does the data follow a simple generator's rule?
    Source classification 8 Given all of the above, what can we honestly say?

    Each statistical test passes when \(p \ge 0.01\). Where a test combines several sub-tests, it reports a Bonferroni or Sidak value, so its single p-value already accounts for the combining.

    How to read the measured results. Each test below reports two measurements.

    False alarms come from calibration: 120 samples of good data, each 1 MiB, half from os.urandom and half from SHA-256 counter mode. We count how many fail at \(p < 0.01\). With only 120 samples the count is rough. Even "0 of 120" is consistent with a true rate of up to 3.0% (95% interval), so read it as "no sign of trouble", not as "never". The counts are from the full rerun on the final code, and they move with chance: test 5 went from 4 of 120 in an earlier run to 1 of 120 here.

    Detections come from a power run: 59 samples, 58 of them with an expected verdict (parity has none). Most are 2 MiB, and 50 of them come from two runs of the author's own corpus (a different seed each time, with the jitter sources re-measured), so counts are samples, not distinct generators. In these chapters:

    • "Raw physical" means the 10 unconditioned physical samples. Four are real CPU timing jitter, and six are simulations of physics, driven by a PRNG.
    • "Weak generators" means the 30 samples labelled as weak PRNGs.
    • "Defective" means one sample: SHA-256 used as a stateless conditioner with repeating input.
    • "Parity" means one public Zenodo dataset: real quantum data from entangled photons, but its authors repeated an 8,000-byte string to fill the file. So it is not a fair test of a QRNG, and most of its flags below come from the repetition.
    • "Flagged" means \(p < 0.01\). The count at the stricter verdict line is added only where it differs.

    Two more samples are real-world PRNG files from Zenodo (GFSR4 and MT19937), and the other 15 are the clean samples listed at the end of Chapter 2. A sample can be flagged by a test without changing its verdict.

    10.2 Bias (test 1)¶

    The idea in plain words. Imagine a slightly bent coin. A few tosses will not show it, but thousands will. We also treat a byte as eight separate seats, each with its own coin, because one bent coin in seat 3 can be hidden when you only look at the overall total.

    Example 10.2: Bias

    Take 100 bits with 62 ones. The exact two-sided binomial p-value is 0.0210. (The normal shortcut gives 0.0164, a fair approximation.) On its own that would pass at 0.01 anyway, and because the test checks 9 things, it would be reported as \(9 \times 0.0210 = 0.189\) if it were the smallest. For the side measurement, suppose the most common byte appears 12 times in 1000 bytes. Then \(\hat{p} = 0.012\), the upper bound is \(p_u = 0.0209\), and the min-entropy estimate is 5.58 bits per byte.

    The mathematics. The whole-file test counts ones among \(n\) bits, and each position test counts ones in one seat across \(N\) bytes:

    \[ S \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, \tfrac{1}{2}), \qquad p_{\text{all}} = \min\bigl(1,\ 2\, P(X \ge \max(S, n-S))\bigr) \]
    \[ z_b = \frac{S_b - N/2}{\sqrt{N/4}}, \qquad p_b = \mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{|z_b|}{\sqrt{2}}\right), \quad b = 0, \dots, 7 \]
    \[ p = \min\bigl(1,\ 9 \min(p_{\text{all}}, p_0, \dots, p_7)\bigr) \]
    • \(n\): the number of bits, equal to \(8N\).
    • \(S\): the number of 1 bits in the whole sample.
    • \(X\): a binomial variable with the same \(n\) and probability one half.
    • \(S_b\): the number of 1 bits at position \(b\) of each byte.
    • \(z_b\): how many standard deviations \(S_b\) sits from \(N/2\).

    The factor 9 is Bonferroni over the 9 sub-tests. The binomial test is exact below \(10^7\) bits. Above that the code uses the normal approximation, \(\mathrm{erfc}\) of the z-score, which at this size is accurate.

    The test also reports, without using it for pass or fail, the SP 800-90B most-common-value min-entropy: \(H = -\log_2 p_u\), with \(p_u = \min(1,\ \hat{p} + 2.576 \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/(N-1)})\), where \(\hat{p}\) is the share of the most common byte. This reproduces the standard's worked example (\(p_u = 0.6895\) gives 0.5363 bits).

    What it catches and misses. False alarms: 1 of 120. It flagged 15 of 59: all 10 raw physical, 4 weak generators (MINSTD and RANDU) and parity. At the verdict line that falls to 13, because the sim-drifting-bits sample only reached \(p = 0.0035\) and \(p = 0.0094\). At 1 MiB, the overall ones fraction has to reach about 0.50056 to fail. It sees balance and nothing else. A file running 0, 1, 2, ..., 255 over and over passes with \(p = 1\), because every seat is balanced, although it is totally predictable. MT19937 and java.util.Random passed too. (A repeating 0101 pattern does fail, because each seat is constant.)

    10.3 Multi-scale entropy (test 2)¶

    The idea in plain words. Read a sentence one letter at a time and it may look fine. Read it in pairs and you notice that "qu" always travels together. This test looks at the bit stream with sixteen different lenses: 1 bit at a time, 2 bits at a time, and so on up to 16.

    Example 10.3: Multi-scale entropy

    Take 80 bits of 01010101... (ten bytes of value 0x55). At 1 bit, there are 40 zeros and 40 ones, so \(X^2 = 0\) and \(p = 1\). It looks perfect. At 2 bits, there are 40 symbols and each of the 4 cells expects 10, but every symbol is "01". The counts are 0, 40, 0, 0, so \(X^2 = (100 + 900 + 100 + 100)/10 = 120\) with 3 degrees of freedom, and \(p = 7.7 \times 10^{-26}\). At 3 bits only 3.25 symbols per cell would be expected, below our minimum of 5, so we stop. Bonferroni over the 2 scales gives \(p = 1.5 \times 10^{-25}\).

    The mathematics. Cut the \(n\) bits into \(m_k = \lfloor n/k \rfloor\) non-overlapping \(k\)-bit symbols, and compare the counts with their expected value:

    \[ X_k^2 = \sum_{j=0}^{2^k - 1} \frac{(O_{k,j} - m_k/2^k)^2}{m_k/2^k} \sim \chi^2(2^k - 1) \]
    \[ p = \min\bigl(1,\ L \min_k p_k\bigr) \]
    • \(k\): the symbol size in bits, from 1 to 16, while at least 5 symbols per cell are expected.
    • \(O_{k,j}\): the number of times the \(k\)-bit symbol \(j\) occurred.
    • \(p_k\): the chi-square tail probability of \(X_k^2\).
    • \(L\): the number of scales used, which is 16 at 1 MiB.

    The test also reports an entropy per bit at each scale, with the Miller-Madow correction (a small top-up, because counting from a finite sample underestimates entropy), but the pass or fail decision uses only the p-value above.

    What it catches and misses. False alarms: 0 of 120. It flagged 15 of 59 (14 at the verdict line): 9 raw physical, 4 weak generators (MINSTD and RANDU), the defective conditioner and parity. It misses order that shows up only beyond 16 bits, and since symbols do not overlap, it looks at only one alignment of the stream at each size.

    We found and fixed a defect here. Our first version used the G-statistic, and it failed 89% of good samples. At \(k = 16\), with about 8 expected counts per cell, G overshoots its chi-square reference by a factor of about 1.02, which is a lot at that scale. Pearson's \(X^2\) has a mean of exactly \(2^k - 1\) under the null, and false alarms dropped to 0 of 120.

    10.4 Correlation analysis (test 3)¶

    The idea in plain words. If today's weather tells you something about tomorrow's, the days are not independent. We ask the same of bytes and bits: does a value say anything about the one that came 1, 2 or 32 steps before it? We also check whether two seats inside a byte tend to move together, like two friends who always vote the same way.

    Example 10.4: Correlation analysis

    Take the ten values 10, 20, 30, ..., 100, a plain upward trend. The sample autocorrelations are \(r_1 = 0.700\) and \(r_2 = 0.412\). With \(n = 10\) and \(h = 2\) lags:

    \[ Q = 10 \times 12 \times \left(\frac{0.700^2}{9} + \frac{0.412^2}{8}\right) = 9.08 \]

    Against \(\chi^2(2)\) this gives \(p = 0.0107\). Ten values is far too few for the chi-square reference to be reliable, so treat this as a picture of the arithmetic only.

    The mathematics. The serial part is the Ljung-Box statistic, and the cross-bit part is Pearson correlation:

    \[ r_k = \frac{\sum_{i} (x_i - \bar{x})(x_{i+k} - \bar{x})}{\sum_{i} (x_i - \bar{x})^2}, \qquad Q = n(n+2) \sum_{k=1}^{h} \frac{r_k^2}{n-k} \sim \chi^2(h) \]
    \[ z_{ab} = r_{ab} \sqrt{N}, \qquad p_{ab} = \mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{|z_{ab}|}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \]
    • \(x_i\): the series, either bytes (first \(2^{21}\) bytes, \(h = 32\)) or bits (from the first \(2^{18}\) bytes, \(h = 64\)).
    • \(r_k\): the correlation between the series and itself shifted by \(k\).
    • \(n\): the length of that series.
    • \(r_{ab}\): the correlation between bit positions \(a\) and \(b\) of each byte.
    • \(N\): the number of bytes used for this, up to \(2^{20}\).

    Bonferroni runs over the 28 position pairs to give \(p_{\text{cross}}\), then the final value is

    \[p = \min(1,\ 3\min(p_{\text{byte}}, p_{\text{bit}}, p_{\text{cross}})).\]

    This is conservative.

    What it catches and misses. False alarms: 1 of 120. It flagged 18 of 59 (17 at the verdict line): all 10 raw physical, 7 weak generators and parity. Six of the weak flags are LCG word streams (the Numerical Recipes LCG, MINSTD and RANDU). The seventh is xorshift128+ at \(p = 0.0072\) on one seed only, and it did not repeat on the other seed, so we would not read much into it. The test sees only straight-line dependence at short lags. Relationships that do not show as a straight line in byte values, such as those in MT19937 or java.util.Random, pass untouched.

    10.5 Entropy stability across windows (test 4)¶

    The idea in plain words. A school's yearly average can look fine while one class had a terrible week. So we mark each 4 KiB page of the data separately, then ask two questions. Do the page marks spread the way chance says they should? And are too many pages terrible?

    Example 10.5: Entropy stability across windows

    A 1 MiB file has 256 windows. Suppose one window gives \(X^2 = 340\). Against \(\chi^2(255)\), \(p = 0.00029\), so it counts as a bad window. By chance we expect \(256 \times 0.001 = 0.256\) bad windows. Seeing 2 bad windows has tail probability 0.0276, which after doubling (for the 2 parts of the test) is 0.0552, a pass. Seeing 3 has tail probability 0.00229, or 0.00458 after doubling, a fail.

    The mathematics. For each window \(w\) of 4096 bytes, with 16 expected per byte value:

    \[ X_w^2 = \sum_{v=0}^{255} \frac{(c_{w,v} - 16)^2}{16}, \qquad p_w = P\bigl(\chi^2(255) \ge X_w^2\bigr) \]
    \[ B = \#\{w : p_w < 10^{-3}\} \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(W, 10^{-3}), \qquad p_{\text{tail}} = P(B' \ge B) \]
    \[ p = \min\bigl(1,\ 2\min(p_{\text{KS}}, p_{\text{tail}})\bigr) \]
    • \(c_{w,v}\): how many times byte value \(v\) occurs in window \(w\).
    • \(p_w\): the window's p-value. Under the null these are independent and uniform between 0 and 1.
    • \(p_{\text{KS}}\): the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test that the \(p_w\) really are uniform.
    • \(W\): the number of windows.
    • \(B\): the number of bad windows, and \(B'\) a fresh binomial variable with the same law.

    Despite the test's name, its p-value uses only these chi-square results. The code also reports each window's entropy as side information, and that plays no part in pass or fail.

    What it catches and misses. False alarms: 1 of 120. It flagged 17 of 59: all 10 raw physical, 6 weak generators (the Numerical Recipes LCG words, MINSTD and RANDU) and parity. It suits local faults, such as a stuck run, a dropout or a burst, and the code tells a local fault from a whole-sample problem by checking whether most windows fail. It checks only byte counts inside each window, so it is blind to the order of bytes. Generators with even byte counts, such as MT19937, pass.

    10.6 Periodicity and predictability (test 5)¶

    The idea in plain words. This test has two parts. The first listens for a hum: if a single note in the data is far louder than all the others, something periodic is going on. The second is a fortune teller who has to write down each guess before seeing the answer, and we simply keep score. Fair bytes give her 1 chance in 256, however clever she is.

    Example 10.6: Periodicity and predictability

    Take 16 values that repeat a period-4 shape with small wobbles: 200, 128, 50, 128, 205, 131, 47, 126, 198, 125, 52, 130, 203, 127, 49, 129. A transform splits the values into frequencies, and each frequency's entry is called an ordinate. With \(m = 7\) ordinates, one holds almost everything: \(g = 0.9985\), and the bound is \(7(1-g)^6 = 8.0 \times 10^{-17}\). A jumbled 16 values we tried gave \(g = 0.493\) and a bound of 0.118. For the fortune teller, take 7, 42, 99 repeated to 60 bytes. The lag predictor gets 56 of 59 guesses right, where chance would give about 0.23, and \(p = 4.4 \times 10^{-131}\).

    The mathematics. The hum part is Fisher's g over the periodogram (the table of how much power each frequency holds) of the mean-removed bytes:

    \[ I_j = \left| \sum_{t=0}^{L-1} (x_t - \bar{x})\, e^{-2\pi i j t / L} \right|^2, \qquad g = \frac{\max_j I_j}{\sum_j I_j}, \qquad P(g > x) \le m (1-x)^{m-1} \]

    The predictor part compares hits with a binomial law:

    \[ H \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, 1/256), \qquad p_{\text{pred}} = P(B \ge H) \]
    \[ p = \min\bigl(1,\ 3\min(p_{\text{Fisher}}, p_{\text{lag}}, p_{\text{Markov}})\bigr) \]
    • \(L\): the number of bytes in the transform, up to \(2^{20}\).
    • \(x_t\), \(\bar{x}\): the byte at position \(t\) and the mean of those bytes.
    • \(i\): the imaginary unit, the square root of minus one.
    • \(I_j\): the power at frequency index \(j\), leaving out both zero frequency and the very top one.
    • \(m\): the number of ordinates, \(L/2 - 1\). Under the null they are asymptotically i.i.d. exponential.
    • \(g\): the share of all the power in the loudest ordinate. The bound is the first term of Fisher's exact series.
    • \(H\): the hits over \(n\) guesses, using up to \(2^{19}\) bytes.
    • \(B\): a binomial variable with the same law as \(H\).

    The lag predictor copies the byte from whichever of 32 lags has scored best so far (SP 800-90B section 6.3.8). The Markov predictor guesses the most common successor seen so far of the previous byte. Each guess uses only the past, so if the bytes are fair, every guess is right with probability \(1/256\) regardless. That is a martingale argument (a fair-game argument: whatever has happened so far, the next step is fair on average), and it is why the binomial law holds. Steps with no prediction yet count as misses, which can only make the test more cautious.

    What it catches and misses. False alarms: 1 of 120, at \(p = 0.0082\), well above the verdict line. An earlier run of the same size gave 4 of 120, the most of any test then, and 500 further samples gave 4 failures, 0.8%, so that high count looks like chance. It flagged 16 of 59: 8 raw physical, 6 weak generators (the Numerical Recipes LCG words, MINSTD and RANDU), the defective conditioner and parity. It missed the simulated photon time-bin source on both seeds. A plain period of 2 falls on the very top frequency, which \(g\) leaves out, so that case relies on the predictors. Strong non-linear generators beat both parts.

    10.7 Shannon entropy test (test 9)¶

    The idea in plain words. Think of a lottery with 256 numbers. Your average surprise is highest when all numbers are equally likely, and any favouritism lowers it. So a small entropy shortfall means uneven byte counts. This is the point people often miss: an entropy test and an evenness test are the same test in different clothes.

    Example 10.7: Shannon entropy test

    Use a toy alphabet of 4 symbols, where the maximum entropy is 2 bits, with counts 30, 20, 25, 25 out of \(n = 100\). The entropy is \(\hat{H} = 1.98548\) bits. Then \(2n \ln 2\, (2 - \hat{H}) = 2.0136\), and computing \(G\) directly from the counts gives the same 2.0136, as the identity promises. With 3 degrees of freedom, \(p = 0.570\).

    Now at full size. Good data of 1 MiB does not reach 8.0 bits. It averages about 7.999825, and that is normal. A value of \(\hat{H} = 7.99982\) gives \(G = 261.7\) and \(p = 0.374\). A value of \(\hat{H} = 7.9997\) sounds equally perfect, but gives \(G = 436.1\) and \(p = 1.2 \times 10^{-11}\).

    The mathematics. For byte counts \(c_v\) adding up to \(n\):

    \[ \hat{H} = -\sum_v \frac{c_v}{n} \log_2 \frac{c_v}{n}, \qquad G = 2n \ln 2\, (8 - \hat{H}) = 2 \sum_v c_v \ln \frac{c_v}{n/256} \sim \chi^2(255) \]
    • \(c_v\): the number of times byte value \(v\) occurs.
    • \(n\): the number of bytes.
    • \(\hat{H}\): the measured Shannon entropy, in bits per byte.
    • \(G\): the likelihood-ratio statistic against the uniform distribution (Wilks' theorem, which says such statistics follow chi-square in large samples, gives its reference).

    The p-value is the upper tail of \(\chi^2(255)\). With 4096 expected per cell, the Williams correction, a multiplier that adjusts \(G\) when counts are small, is 1.00004, which is negligible.

    What it catches and misses. False alarms: 0 of 120, and the p-values look uniform. It flagged 13 of 59: 8 raw physical, 4 weak generators (MINSTD and RANDU) and parity. It looks at byte counts only. A file running 0, 1, 2, ..., 255 over and over has perfect counts, so \(G = 0\) and \(p = 1\), despite being totally predictable. So reading "7.9999 bits per byte" on a datasheet tells you very little on its own.

    10.8 Compression (test 10)¶

    The idea in plain words. Try to give everyone in a big town a short nickname. There simply are not enough short names to go round, so most people must keep long ones. Compression works the same way: very few strings can have a short description. So if a real compressor shrinks a file noticeably, the file was almost certainly not random. We do not need any statistics to say this, only counting.

    Example 10.8: Compression

    Counting first. Take strings of \(N = 4\) bits (16 of them), and a compressor whose output tells the decoder where to stop. Then no description is the start of another, a property called prefix-free. Compressing by \(s = 2\) bits means a description of at most 2 bits, and a prefix-free set can hold at most 4 of those (for example 00, 01, 10, 11). So at most 4 of the 16 strings can compress that far, which is exactly the bound of \(2^{-2} = 1/4\). Drop the prefix-free rule and there are 7 descriptions of at most 2 bits, counting the empty one, so the rule matters.

    Now real compressors, on 1000 bytes. "ABCD" repeated 250 times compresses to 20 bytes with zlib. Then \(s = 8(1000 - 20 - 64) = 7328\) bits, and the bound is so small it rounds to 0. For 1000 random bytes, zlib gives 1011 bytes and lzma 1060, so no saving, and \(p = 1\). A saving of just 1 byte beyond the allowance gives \(s = 8\) and \(p = 0.0078\), which fails at 0.01.

    The mathematics. Fix a decompressor whose files are self-delimiting, so that its descriptions are prefix-free. Both of ours qualify: we checked that the zlib and lzma decoders stop at their own end marker and leave any trailing bytes unread. Kraft's inequality says the lengths of a prefix-free set satisfy \(\sum_d 2^{-|d|} \le 1\), so at most \(2^{N-s}\) descriptions can have \(N - s\) bits or fewer. There are \(2^N\) strings of \(N\) bits, so:

    \[ P(\text{random data compresses by at least } s \text{ bits}) \le 2^{-s} \]
    \[ s = 8(n - c - 64), \qquad p = \begin{cases} 1 & s \le 0 \\ \min(1,\ 2 \cdot 2^{-s}) & s > 0 \end{cases} \]
    • \(n\): the bytes tested, up to the first \(2^{21}\).
    • \(c\): the smallest compressed size, from lzma (preset 6) and zlib (level 9).
    • 64: the bytes we allow for container overhead before counting any saving.
    • \(s\): the saving in bits.
    • The factor 2: Bonferroni, because we tried two compressors.

    This is an exact counting argument, not an approximation.

    What it catches and misses. False alarms: 0 of 120 (every good sample gets \(p = 1\)). It flagged 12 of 59: 6 raw physical, 4 weak generators (the Numerical Recipes LCG words and RANDU), the defective conditioner and parity. Its strength is that a failure is close to unarguable. Its weakness is that general compressors are not looking for arithmetic structure. MT19937, java.util.Random and most weak generators pass, as do several raw physical samples, such as the simulated photon source.

    During verification we found the bound was being applied once, although two compressors were tried. It never caused a false alarm, but it was unjustified, so we added the Bonferroni factor of 2.

    10.9 Summary¶

    • The bias test checks the whole-file balance and each of the eight bit positions; multi-scale entropy applies a chi-square at 1 to 16 bits at a time; correlation combines Ljung-Box on bytes and bits with cross-bit correlations.
    • Entropy stability checks each 4 KiB window; periodicity combines Fisher's \(g\) with predictors whose hits are exactly binomial.
    • The Shannon entropy test is the same test as the evenness test in different clothes: \(G=2n\ln2\,(8-\hat H)\sim\chi^2(255)\). A reading of "7.9999 bits per byte" on a datasheet tells you very little on its own.
    • Compression is a counting argument: at most \(2^{N-s}\) of the \(2^N\) strings can be compressed by \(s\) bits, so a real compressor that shrinks a file noticeably proves it is not random.
    • Three defects were found and fixed in this suite (the G statistic at 16-bit symbols, the handling of ties in the runs formula, and a missing Bonferroni factor for two compressors). Each is a lesson in why calibration on known-good data is essential.

    10.10 Exercises¶

    1. Take 100 bits with 62 ones. Find the exact two-sided binomial p-value, and say whether it fails at \(\alpha=0.01\). What would the bias test report if this were the smallest of its 9 sub-tests?

      Answer

      The exact two-sided value is \(2P(X\ge62)=0.0210\), which does not fail at 0.01. Because the bias test checks 9 things (the whole file and eight positions), a smallest sub-test p-value of 0.0210 is reported as \(9\times0.0210=0.189\).

    2. A source has byte counts 30, 20, 25, 25 over a toy alphabet of four symbols (\(n=100\)). Compute \(\hat H\), then \(G=2n\ln2(2-\hat H)\) and the p-value on 3 degrees of freedom.

      Answer

      \(\hat H=1.9855\) bits. Then \(G=2\times100\times0.6931\times(2-1.9855)=2.0136\), which equals \(2\sum c_v\ln(c_v/25)\), as the identity promises. On \(\chi^2(3)\), \(p=0.570\).

    3. A 1000-byte file compresses to 930 bytes with its best compressor. Using \(s=8(n-c-64)\) and \(p=\min(1,2\cdot2^{-s})\) for \(s>0\), find \(s\) and \(p\). What if it compresses to 20 bytes?

      Answer

      For \(c=930\): \(s=8(1000-930-64)=48\) bits, so \(p=2\times2^{-48}=7.1\times10^{-15}\), a clear failure. For \(c=20\): \(s=8(1000-20-64)=7328\), and the bound is so small that it rounds to 0. If \(c\ge936\) then \(s\le0\) and \(p=1\).

    4. Why does a datasheet figure of "7.9999 bits per byte of entropy" tell you very little about a generator?

      Answer

      It is a Shannon entropy computed from byte counts only. A file running 0, 1, 2, ..., 255 over and over has perfect counts and scores 8.0, yet is totally predictable. Also, good 1 MiB data does not reach 8.0 (it averages about 7.999825), and 7.9997 gives \(p=1.2\times10^{-11}\) even though it sounds equally perfect.

    5. What was wrong with the first version of the multi-scale entropy test, and what did the fix teach?

      Answer

      It used the G-statistic, which failed 89% of good samples at 16-bit symbols, where only about 8 counts per cell are expected and G overshoots its chi-square reference by about 2%. Pearson's \(X^2\) has mean exactly \(2^k-1\) under the null, and using it brought false alarms to 0 of 120. The lesson is to measure false alarms on known-good data before trusting a test.
