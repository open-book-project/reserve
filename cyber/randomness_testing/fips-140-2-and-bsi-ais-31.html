    Chapter 7: FIPS 140-2 and BSI AIS 31¶

    Two standards come from the world of certification rather than research. The FIPS 140-2 power-up tests are four quick checks on 20,000 bits, designed to run inside a device at start-up; they were struck out of the standard in 2002 but remain widely known. BSI's AIS 20 and AIS 31 are the evaluation method for random number generators in the German Common Criteria scheme, and they take a different view altogether: statistical tests are not enough for a physical generator, which needs a stochastic model and on-board tests. This chapter covers both.

    7.1 About the FIPS 140-2 power-up tests¶

    FIPS 140-2 (May 2001) is the US standard for cryptographic modules. Its section 4.9.1 originally listed four quick statistical checks on one stream of 20,000 bits from each generator, meant to run inside a device at power-up or on demand. There are no p-values here: each test has fixed limits, and a block either passes or fails. They do not have a long life in the standard. Change Notice 1 (10 October 2001) corrected the runs intervals, and Change Notice 2 (3 December 2002) struck all four tests out of section 4.9.1, along with the related requirement in section 4.7.1, so the statistical tests are no longer FIPS 140-2 requirements. We include them because they remain widely known and because BSI's T1 to T4 descend from the same family in FIPS 140-1, with wider limits.

    7.2 Monobit (FIPS 140-2)¶

    The idea in plain words. Count the ones in 20,000 bits. A fair source gives close to 10,000. The limits are wide enough that a healthy device will almost never trip them. It is a sanity check, like asking whether a coin has two heads.

    Example 7.2: Monobit (FIPS 140-2)

    A block with 10,100 ones passes, as \(9725 < 10100 < 10275\). A block with 10,275 ones fails, since the limits are strict. The limit is about 3.89 standard deviations out, as \(\sigma = \sqrt{5000} = 70.71\).

    The mathematics.

    \[X = \sum_{i=1}^{20000} b_i, \qquad \text{pass if } 9725 < X < 10275\]
    • \(b_i\) is bit \(i\), and \(X\) is the count of ones.
    • Under a fair source, \(X\) is Binomial(20000, 1/2). We compute a false-failure rate of about \(1.03 \times 10^{-4}\) per block.

    The standard gives only the limits, with no p-value.

    What it catches and misses. It catches a dead or badly biased source at start-up. A pattern as simple as 0101... passes. So it is an alarm for disasters, not a test of quality.

    7.3 Poker (FIPS 140-2)¶

    The idea in plain words. Cut the block into 5000 pieces of 4 bits and count each of the 16 possible values. They should appear about equally often, but not too exactly: real randomness has some wobble. So the test fails a block that is too lumpy, and also one that is too even.

    Example 7.3: Poker (FIPS 140-2)

    If the counts are eight values at 300 and eight at 325, \(X = 8.0\), which passes. If they are all 312 or 313, \(X = 0.0128\), below 2.16, so the block fails as too even. If one value fills all 5000 slots, \(X = 75000\).

    The mathematics.

    \[X = \frac{16}{5000} \sum_{i=0}^{15} f(i)^2 - 5000, \qquad \text{pass if } 2.16 < X < 46.17\]
    • \(f(i)\) is the count of 4-bit value \(i\).
    • \(X\) is approximately chi-square with 15 degrees of freedom. On that basis, the limits leave about \(5 \times 10^{-5}\) in each tail, by our calculation.

    What it catches and misses. It catches bias in short patterns and sources that are suspiciously regular. It looks only at aligned 4-bit pieces, so a defect that lies across piece boundaries may be missed.

    7.4 Runs (FIPS 140-2)¶

    The idea in plain words. Count the runs of each length, separately for ones and zeros. About half of all runs should be length 1, a quarter length 2, and so on. There are 12 counts and each must land in its own interval, so it checks the whole shape, not just the total.

    Example 7.4: Runs (FIPS 140-2)

    The expected number of runs of length 1 of one value is \((20000 - 1 + 3)/2^3 = 2500.25\), comfortably in the middle of 2315 to 2685. A block with 2700 runs of length 1 fails.

    The mathematics.

    Run length Required interval, corrected by Change Notice 1 Interval first published
    1 2315 to 2685 2343 to 2657
    2 1114 to 1386 1135 to 1365
    3 527 to 723 542 to 708
    4 240 to 384 251 to 373
    5 103 to 209 111 to 201
    6 or more 103 to 209 111 to 201
    \[r(n, i) = \frac{n - i + 3}{2^{i+2}}\]
    • All 12 counts, for ones and for zeros, must lie in the interval for their length. Runs longer than 6 count as length 6.
    • \(r(n, i)\) is the expected number of runs of length \(i\) of one value in \(n\) bits, a formula quoted in BSI's document.

    What it catches and misses. It catches sticky or over-alternating sources. Since it counts runs without regard to position, it is blind to where they fall in the block.

    7.5 Long run (FIPS 140-2)¶

    The idea in plain words. If 26 or more identical bits appear in a row anywhere in the block, the test fails. In a good source that is rare, so a long run strongly suggests something has stuck, even briefly. It is the simplest of the four.

    Example 7.5: Long run (FIPS 140-2)

    A run of 25 passes, and a run of 26 fails. For either bit value, the expected number of such runs in 20,000 bits is about \(20000 \cdot 2^{-26} = 0.0003\), so about three good blocks in 10,000 would fail by chance.

    The mathematics.

    \[\text{pass if } \max_{\text{runs}} (\text{run length}) < 26\]
    • Runs of zeros and of ones both count.
    • The 0.0003 above is our own estimate, not a number from the standard.

    What it catches and misses. It catches a source that sticks briefly, which an average would hide. It tells you nothing about anything shorter than 26 bits, and it would not catch a source that fails slowly.

    7.6 About BSI AIS 31¶

    BSI, Germany's federal cybersecurity office, uses AIS 20 and AIS 31 as the evaluation method for random number generators in its Common Criteria certification scheme. Common Criteria is the international framework for evaluating the security of IT products. The tests here come from the technical document by Killmann and Schindler, version 2.0 (18 September 2011). Its core idea differs from everything above: statistical tests are not enough for a physical generator. You also need a stochastic model of the noise source and on-board tests. It defines classes such as PTG.1, PTG.2 and PTG.3 for physical generators, DRG.1 to DRG.4 for deterministic ones, and NTG.1 for non-physical true generators. Tests have fixed limits and a retest rule, and T0 to T8 are grouped into procedures A and B below.

    An important update: version 3.0 (Peter and Schindler, September 2024) replaces this arrangement. It removes class PTG.1 and allows min-entropy claims. Its black-box suite for PTG.2 has four tests on 2,040,000 bits: monobit (pass from 9655 to 10345), poker (\(1.03 < t < 57.4\)), and the MultiMMC and LZ78Y predictors from SP 800-90B, on \(10^6\) bits each, failing below 0.95. Version 3.0 calls this suite a sanity check and says passing alone does not warrant a meaningful statement about quality. Read T0 to T8 as the 2011 scheme.

    7.7 T0: disjointness test¶

    The idea in plain words. Take 65,536 words of 48 bits. There are so many possible 48-bit words that two matching words should almost never happen. If any two are the same, the test fails. It is the birthday problem with a very long year.

    Example 7.7: T0: disjointness test

    The number of pairs is \(2^{16}(2^{16} - 1)/2\), and each pair matches with chance \(2^{-48}\). So the chance of a match on ideal data is about \(7.63 \times 10^{-6}\), which is the document's \(2^{-17}\).

    The mathematics.

    \[\text{pass if and only if } w_1, \ldots, w_{2^{16}} \text{ are all different}, \qquad w_i \in \{0, 1\}^{48}\]
    • \(w_i\) are the 48-bit words, taken from consecutive outputs.
    • The rejection probability for an ideal generator is \(2^{-17}\).

    What it catches and misses. It catches short cycles and repeated output, and very small effective state. It reads only 65,536 words, though, so a generator whose period is just above that passes as long as no word repeats within the cycle, however weak its statistics are.

    7.8 T1: monobit test¶

    The idea in plain words. Count ones in 20,000 bits, just like the FIPS test, but with wider limits. BSI wants the chance of a false alarm in a single test to be about one in a million, because this test is repeated 257 times.

    Example 7.8: T1: monobit test

    A block with 10,300 ones fails FIPS 140-2 but passes T1, since \(9654 < 10300 < 10346\). At the top of the band, the share of ones is 0.5173, for which the document gives a min-entropy of at least 0.9509 and a Shannon entropy of at least 0.9991.

    The mathematics.

    \[T_1 = \sum_{j=1}^{20000} b_j, \qquad \text{pass if and only if } 9654 < T_1 < 10346\]
    • \(b_j\) is bit \(j\).
    • We compute the false-failure rate on ideal data as about \(1.03 \times 10^{-6}\) per block.

    What it catches and misses. It catches gross bias. Its limits are too wide for mild bias, which is why the 257 repetitions and the stricter T6 exist. It is blind to order.

    7.9 T2: poker test¶

    The idea in plain words. Same as the FIPS poker test: count the 16 possible 4-bit values across 5000 pieces. Too lumpy fails, and so does too even. Again, the limits are wider so that good data rarely trips the alarm.

    Example 7.9: T2: poker test

    Counts of eight values at 300 and eight at 325 give \(T_2 = 8.0\), inside the band. Perfectly even counts of 312 and 313 give 0.0128, below 1.03, and fail.

    The mathematics.

    \[T_2 = \frac{16}{5000} \sum_{i=0}^{15} f[i]^2 - 5000, \qquad \text{pass if } 1.03 < T_2 < 57.4\]
    • \(f[i]\) counts the 4-bit value \(i\), built as \(c_j = 8b_{4j-3} + 4b_{4j-2} + 2b_{4j-1} + b_{4j}\).
    • For an ideal generator, \(T_2\) is chi-square with 15 degrees of freedom. The document gives a rejection probability of \(1.014 \times 10^{-6}\), which our calculation reproduces.

    What it catches and misses. It catches short-pattern bias and excess regularity. It is limited to aligned 4-bit pieces.

    7.10 T3: runs test¶

    The idea in plain words. Same idea as the FIPS runs test: 12 counts of runs of each length and value, each inside its own interval. It checks the whole shape of the run-length distribution, with limits set for a one-in-a-million false alarm rate.

    Example 7.10: T3: runs test

    The expected count for length 1 is about 2500, so a block with 2740 runs of length 1 fails, as it is over the limit of 2733. A block with 2700 fails FIPS 140-2, whose corrected limit is 2685, but passes here.

    The mathematics.

    Run length Lower bound Upper bound
    1 2267 2733
    2 1079 1421
    3 502 748
    4 223 402
    5 90 223
    6 or more 90 223
    • \(T_3(\ell, p)\) is the number of runs of length \(\ell\) of value \(p\). Runs longer than 5 are summed together into the last row.
    • All 12 counts must lie within their bounds. The rejection probability is about \(10^{-6}\).

    What it catches and misses. It catches over-alternating or sticky behaviour. It does not see where runs fall, nor relations between one run and the next.

    7.11 T4: long run test¶

    The idea in plain words. If any run of 34 or more identical bits appears, the block fails. It is the same simple alarm as in FIPS, with a longer limit to match this standard's lower false-alarm rate.

    Example 7.11: T4: long run test

    A run of 33 passes, and a run of 34 fails. By the same estimate as before, the expected number on good data is \(20000 \cdot 2^{-34} = 1.16 \times 10^{-6}\).

    The mathematics.

    \[\text{pass if and only if } T_3(\ell, p) = 0 \text{ for all } \ell \ge 34 \text{ and } p \in \{0, 1\}\]
    • \(T_3(\ell, p)\) is the count of runs of length \(\ell\) and value \(p\).
    • The document gives a rejection probability of about \(10^{-6}\).

    What it catches and misses. It catches a source that sticks for at least 34 bits. Shorter stuck periods can pass, and repeated runs of 33 would not be flagged here.

    7.12 T5: autocorrelation test¶

    The idea in plain words. Compare a sequence with a shifted copy of itself. If the bits are independent, they should agree about half the time, whatever the shift. If agreement at some shift is way off, the source has a memory or rhythm. This is the only test in T1 to T5 that looks directly at dependence at a distance.

    Example 7.12: T5: autocorrelation test

    Over 5000 comparisons, we expect 2500 with \(\sigma = \sqrt{1250} = 35.36\). The limits 2326 and 2674 sit 174 away, about 4.92 standard deviations. So a count of 2700 at some shift fails.

    The mathematics.

    \[T_5(\tau) = \sum_{j=1}^{5000} \left(b_j \oplus b_{j+\tau}\right), \qquad \text{pass if and only if } 2326 < T_5(\tau) < 2674\]
    • \(\tau\) is the shift, from 1 to 5000, and \(\oplus\) is exclusive or.
    • \(b_1\) to \(b_{10000}\) are the 10,000 input bits.
    • In procedure A, the worst shift on the first half of a block is chosen, and then tested on the second half, so that picking the worst shift does not bias the result.
    • The rejection probability is about \(10^{-6}\) per test.

    What it catches and misses. It catches periodic structure and memory, up to a lag of 5000 bits. It sees only linear pairwise agreement, so a higher-order dependence that leaves each pair balanced would slip through.

    7.13 T6: uniform distribution test¶

    The idea in plain words. Count how often each \(k\)-bit word appears as a share of the total, and check that every share stays within a small band around the fair value. It is a direct, strict check of frequency. In procedure B, this is the first test applied to raw bits.

    Example 7.13: T6: uniform distribution test

    With \(k = 1\), \(n = 100{,}000\) and \(a = 0.025\), the share of ones must lie between 0.475 and 0.525. A share of 0.53 fails. On unbiased data, the band is about 15.8 standard deviations wide on each side, so a failure means a real bias.

    The mathematics.

    \[T_6(x) = \frac{\lvert \{j : w_j = x\} \rvert}{n}, \qquad \text{pass if } T_6(x) \in \left[2^{-k} - a,\ 2^{-k} + a\right] \text{ for all } x \in \{0, 1\}^k\]
    • \(w_j\) are the \(k\)-bit words, and \(n\) is how many there are.
    • \(a\) is the allowed tolerance.

    What it catches and misses. It catches meaningful bias in raw bits, which is what matters for a physical source. This band is wide by design, as AIS 31 expects a slightly biased raw source. Small biases that are harmless to entropy pass, and it says nothing about dependence.

    7.14 T7: comparative test (homogeneity)¶

    The idea in plain words. Do two sets of data come from the same distribution? Here that question is asked about transitions. For example: does the next bit behave the same after context "00" as after "10"? If so, the bit two steps back has no further effect. This lets the source have a small one-step memory while ruling out longer ones.

    Example 7.14: T7: comparative test (homogeneity)

    Suppose the next bit gives 50,200 and 49,800 in one context, and 49,700 and 50,300 in the other, with 100,000 each. Then \(T_7 = 5.00\), which is below the limit of 15.137 at \(\alpha = 0.0001\) on 1 degree of freedom, so it passes.

    The mathematics.

    \[p_t = \frac{f_1[t] + \cdots + f_h[t]}{hn}, \qquad T_7(h, s) = \sum_{i=1}^{h} \sum_{t=0}^{s-1} \frac{(f_i[t] - np_t)^2}{np_t}\]
    • \(h\) is the number of samples compared, \(s\) the number of outcomes, and \(n\) the number of trials per sample.
    • \(f_i[t]\) counts outcome \(t\) in sample \(i\), and \(p_t\) is the pooled share.
    • The test fails if \(T_7\) exceeds the chi-square bound with \((h-1)(s-1)\) degrees of freedom at level \(\alpha\).

    What it catches and misses. It catches dependence on more than one earlier bit, up to the contexts tested. It only compares contexts with each other, so if both are biased in the same way, it would not notice.

    7.15 T8: entropy estimation¶

    The idea in plain words. This one, due to Coron, refines Maurer's universal test. For each 8-bit word, look at how far back it last appeared, and turn that distance into an entropy score. Under the right assumptions, the average is an estimate of entropy per word. BSI requires that it comes out close to the full 8 bits.

    Example 7.15: T8: entropy estimation

    The function \(g\) gives \(g(1) = 0\), \(g(2) = 1.4427\), \(g(3) = 2.1640\) and \(g(5) = 3.0056\). If four test words had distances 3, 1, 5, 6, then \(f_C = (2.1640 + 0 + 3.0056 + 3.2942)/4 = 2.1160\). That is far too short and far too low for the real test, which uses \(K = 256{,}000\) words.

    The mathematics.

    \[f_C = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{n=Q+1}^{Q+K} g(A_n), \qquad g(i) = \frac{1}{\ln 2} \sum_{k=1}^{i-1} \frac{1}{k}\]
    • \(A_n\) is the distance back to the last occurrence of word \(w_n\), or \(n\) if there is none.
    • \(L\) is the word length, and \(Q\) and \(K\) are the warm-up and test counts.
    • Procedure B uses \(L = 8\), \(Q = 2560\) and \(K = 256{,}000\), and passes if \(f_C > 7.976\).
    • That limit is \(8 \times 0.997\), tying in with the 0.997 bits of Shannon entropy that class PTG.2 requires per bit.
    • For independent bits, the expected value is \(L\) times the entropy per bit.

    What it catches and misses. It gives an entropy estimate, but only if the source is stationary with a finite memory and the transition effects are negligible. It is a Shannon-style estimate, not min-entropy, so it can sit above the attacker's best guess.

    7.16 Procedure A¶

    The idea in plain words. Procedure A checks that outputs look statistically unremarkable. It runs T0 once, then T1 to T5 on each of 257 blocks. It is for all kinds of generators, applied to internal random numbers, and it checks the traces of each bit position as well as the whole words.

    Example 7.16: Procedure A

    The total input is \(2^{16} \times 48 = 3{,}145{,}728\) bits for T0, plus \(20000 \times 257 = 5{,}140{,}000\) bits for the rest. If one of those tests fails, you run the whole procedure once more on new data. If two fail, the procedure fails outright.

    The mathematics.

    \[\text{number of basic tests} = 1 + 257 \times 5 = 1286\]
    • The document prints 1285 beside this sum, which we take as a slip.
    • Passing all basic tests passes the procedure, which happens with probability 0.9987 for an ideal generator.
    • If more than one fails, the procedure fails.
    • If exactly one fails, you repeat once on fresh data, and any failure in the repeat fails the procedure.

    What it catches and misses. It catches statistical defects in the output and in individual bit positions. Output from a well-seeded software generator passes, so it says nothing about true entropy.

    7.17 Procedure B¶

    The idea in plain words. Procedure B is mainly for raw bits from a physical source, and its target is entropy per bit. A small bias and slight one-step dependence are allowed, but dependence further back is not. Think of it as checking that a coin may be slightly unfair, and slightly sticky, but has no longer memory.

    Example 7.17: Procedure B

    The five steps need at least 100,000, then 400,000, 1,200,000 and 3,200,000 bits, then 2,068,480 bits for T8, a total of 6,968,480 bits if every class fills evenly. A biased source needs more, because the input is not fixed in advance.

    The mathematics.

    Step Test Rule
    1 T6 with \((k, n, a) = (1, 100000, 0.025)\) as T6
    2 one-step transitions from 100,000 pairs per first bit \(\lvert \nu_{emp(0)}(1) + \nu_{emp(1)}(0) - 1 \rvert < 0.02\)
    3 T7 on 2-step transitions, for each middle bit \(\alpha = 0.0001\), 2 comparisons
    4 T7 on 3-step transitions, for each pair of middle bits \(\alpha = 0.0001\), 4 comparisons
    5 T8 with \(L = 8\), \(Q = 2560\), \(K = 256000\) \(f_C > 7.976\)
    • \(\nu_{emp(r)}(i)\) is the observed chance of bit \(i\) following bit \(r\).
    • That gives \(1 + 1 + 2 + 4 + 1 = 9\) basic tests. All passing passes the procedure, with probability 0.9998 for an ideal generator.
    • The retest rule is the same as for procedure A.

    What it catches and misses. It catches low entropy and longer-term dependence in raw data. It cannot on its own prove entropy, because the T8 estimate relies on assumptions that the stochastic model has to justify.

    7.18 Stochastic model requirement¶

    The idea in plain words. This is not a test but a requirement, and it is the heart of AIS 31 for classes PTG.2 and PTG.3. You must explain mathematically how the noise source makes randomness, and from that derive a lower bound on entropy. Tests of data alone can never establish that bound, as a well-made deterministic generator passes them too.

    Example 7.18: Stochastic model requirement

    Coin tossing is the simplest teaching case, and version 3.0 opens its examples with it. If each bit is independent with bias \(\varepsilon\), so that \(P(1) = 0.5 + \varepsilon\), then the entropy per bit is a known function of \(\varepsilon\). At \(\varepsilon = 0.0173\), the Shannon entropy per bit is 0.99914. The model turns one measurable parameter into an entropy bound. Models of real noise circuits are much harder.

    The mathematics.

    \[H_1 \approx n\left(1 - \frac{2\varepsilon^2}{\ln 2}\right) \text{ for small bias}, \qquad H_{min}(n, \varepsilon) = -n\log_2(0.5 + \lvert \varepsilon \rvert)\]
    • \(n\) is the number of independent bits, and \(\varepsilon\) is the bias as above.
    • \(H_1\) is Shannon entropy and \(H_{min}\) is min-entropy. These are the forms in the document's monobit discussion, and they match its figures for T1.
    • In general, the model gives a family of distributions with parameters that can be estimated from samples.
    • Class PTG.2 requires average Shannon entropy above 0.997 per internal random bit.

    What it catches and misses. It addresses what black-box testing cannot: why the numbers should be unpredictable at all. A model is only as good as its assumptions, so the standard requires checking it against data, and it depends on the true physical design being known.

    7.19 Online and total-failure tests¶

    The idea in plain words. A generator can break while in the field, so AIS 31 requires alarms in the device itself. A total-failure test detects a complete breakdown of the noise source, and output must stop immediately. An online test looks for intolerable statistical defects as the device runs. These are the same kind of idea as SP 800-90B's health tests.

    Example 7.19: Online and total-failure tests

    Suppose an amplifier in a noise circuit dies, so the digitiser gives all zeros. A tot test built on run length would catch that quickly, and the device must then stop producing output. Each design chooses its own tests to fit its failure modes, and section 5.5 of the document shows worked examples.

    The mathematics. The document does not fix one formula, because the tests depend on the design. Its requirements are as follows.

    • The tot test must detect a total failure of the source quickly, and it must prevent further output. Some latency is allowed if, for example, entropy is stored in the internal state.
    • The online test may run on raw numbers (for PTG.2 and PTG.3) or on internal numbers (allowed in PTG.1).
    • PTG.1 needs a tot test and an online test, and PTG.2 adds a stochastic model and raw-data tests.

    What it catches and misses. These tests catch breakdowns during operation, which a one-off lab evaluation never could. They catch only the failures their designers foresaw, and an online test after heavy post-processing may see nothing wrong even when the source has died, which is why tests on raw numbers are preferred.

    7.20 Summary¶

    • The FIPS 140-2 tests (monobit, poker, runs, long run) have fixed pass limits on one 20,000-bit block, not p-values. They were removed by Change Notice 2 (3 December 2002), but BSI's T1 to T4 descend from the same family, with wider limits.
    • The limits are chosen so that a good source fails very rarely: about \(1.03\times10^{-4}\) per block for the monobit test and about \(10^{-6}\) for the poker and long-run tests.
    • AIS 31 (version 2.0, 2011) groups tests T0 to T8 into procedure A (for deterministic generators, about 8.3 million bits) and procedure B (for physical generators, including the entropy estimate T8).
    • AIS 31's core idea is the stochastic model: the vendor must justify, from the physics of the device, why the entropy claim holds, and online and total-failure tests must catch breakdowns in operation.
    • Version 3.0 (2024) removes class PTG.1, allows min-entropy claims, and calls its black-box suite a sanity check.

    7.21 Exercises¶

    1. A 20,000-bit block has 10,280 ones. Does it pass the FIPS 140-2 monobit test (limits \(9725<X<10275\))? What about the AIS 31 T1 test (limits \(9654<X<10346\))?

      Answer

      It fails FIPS 140-2, because 10,280 is not below 10,275. It passes T1, because \(9654<10{,}280<10{,}346\). This shows that the AIS 31 limits are wider than those of FIPS 140-2.

    2. How many standard deviations from the mean is the upper FIPS 140-2 monobit limit?

      Answer

      The number of ones in 20,000 fair bits has mean 10,000 and standard deviation \(\sqrt{5000}=70.71\). The limit 10,275 is \(275/70.71=3.89\) standard deviations from the mean.

    3. Why were the FIPS 140-2 statistical tests struck out of the standard, and why does the book still describe them?

      Answer

      Change Notice 2 (3 December 2002) struck all four tests out of section 4.9.1, along with the related requirement in section 4.7.1, so they are no longer FIPS 140-2 requirements. They remain widely known, and BSI's T1 to T4 descend from the same family.

    4. Explain why AIS 31 insists on a stochastic model, and give the simplest example of one.

      Answer

      Statistical tests on output cannot say why the numbers should be unpredictable. The stochastic model explains from the physics of the noise source why the entropy claim holds, turning measurable parameters into an entropy bound. The simplest example is coin tossing: if each bit is independent with \(P(1)=0.5+\varepsilon\), the entropy per bit is a known function of \(\varepsilon\); at \(\varepsilon=0.0173\) the Shannon entropy is 0.99914 bits.

    5. A noise-circuit amplifier dies and the digitiser outputs only zeros. Which kind of AIS 31 test should catch this, and why must it run on raw numbers?

      Answer

      A total-failure test (one of the online tests) built on run length should catch it quickly, and the device must then stop producing output. It should run on raw numbers because after heavy post-processing the output may look fine even when the source has died.
