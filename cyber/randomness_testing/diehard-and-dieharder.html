    Chapter 6: Diehard and Dieharder¶

    Diehard was written in 1995 as a deliberately varied stress test. Its idea is to simulate situations whose answer is known: if the numbers are uniform, the count of parked cars or the closest pair of points follows a known law. Dieharder, its open and extensible successor, reads from a generator directly, draws far more p-values and rates its own tests. This chapter describes the Diehard tests and the four RGB tests added by Dieharder, with the same four-part presentation as before, and explains how to read hundreds of p-values without being fooled.

    6.1 About Diehard¶

    George Marsaglia of Florida State University released Diehard in 1995 with a CD-ROM of random numbers, as a tough, varied stress test for generators. It reads a binary file of about 10 to 12 million bytes as 32-bit integers. Most tests return a p-value that should be uniform on \([0, 1)\), and many combine repeated runs with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test (see Chapter 2). Marsaglia's own advice on reading the output is worth remembering. Hundreds of p-values come out, so a value below 0.025 or above 0.975 is not a failure by itself. The normal approximations behind many of them are weakest in the tails. A stream that truly fails big gives p-values of 0 or 1 to six or more places.

    6.2 Birthday spacings¶

    The idea in plain words. Pick random birthdays in a very long year and sort them. The gaps between neighbouring birthdays should rarely match each other. It is a twist on the classic birthday puzzle, and generators whose values fall on a regular lattice give far too many matching gaps.

    Example 6.2: Birthday spacings

    Take 6 birthdays in a 32-day year: 3, 9, 14, 20, 27, 31. The gaps are 6, 5, 6, 7, 4, and only the value 6 occurs more than once, so \(j = 1\). Here \(\lambda = 6^3/(4 \cdot 32) = 1.6875\), although a year this short is far from the real conditions.

    The mathematics.

    \[\lambda = \frac{m^3}{4n}, \qquad j \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda) \text{ approximately}, \qquad \chi^2 = \sum_k \frac{(\mathrm{obs}_k - \mathrm{exp}_k)^2}{\mathrm{exp}_k}\]
    • \(m\) is the number of birthdays, \(n\) the length of the year, and \(j\) the number of gap values that occur more than once.
    • The written description uses \(m = 512\) and \(n = 2^{24}\), so \(\lambda = 2\), with 500 values of \(j\).
    • The Diehard report printed by the program uses \(m = 1024\), so \(\lambda = 16\), with 17 degrees of freedom.

    The chi-square test runs on nine bit windows, bits 1 to 24 through 9 to 32, and a KS test combines the nine p-values.

    What it catches and misses. It catches lattice structure, including linear congruential and some lagged-Fibonacci generators. Bit windows outside its nine positions are not examined, and it says nothing about order in time, because the data is sorted.

    6.3 Overlapping 5-permutations (OPERM5)¶

    The idea in plain words. Look at five numbers in a row and rank them from smallest to largest. There are 120 possible orderings, and each should be equally common. Then move along by one number and do it again. Because the windows overlap, neighbouring results are linked, and the test has to allow for that.

    Example 6.3: Overlapping 5-permutations (OPERM5)

    The five values 0.3, 0.7, 0.1, 0.9, 0.5 have rank pattern 2, 4, 1, 5, 3, one of \(5! = 120\) orderings, each with probability \(1/120 = 0.00833\). In a million windows, you expect about 8333 of each.

    The mathematics.

    \[Q = (c - \mu)^{T} \Sigma^{+} (c - \mu)\]
    • \(c\) holds the 120 ordering counts, and \(\mu\) their expected values.
    • \(\Sigma^{+}\) is the weak inverse of the \(120 \times 120\) covariance matrix of the counts. A weak inverse is the nearest thing to an inverse when, as here, the matrix cannot be inverted outright.
    • Diehard states rank 99 and compares \(Q\) with a chi-square on 99 degrees of freedom, on two samples of 1,000,000 integers.

    Robert Brown's dieharder notes that the true rank is \(5! - 4! = 96\), says the original test almost certainly had errors, and uses 96.

    What it catches and misses. It catches ordering preferences among close neighbours. It is blind to the actual sizes of values, only their order. Treat Diehard's own version with care: dieharder rates the original test Suspect, for failing even good generators.

    6.4 Binary rank 31x31¶

    The idea in plain words. Build square grids of bits from the generator's numbers and ask how many rows are truly independent, with arithmetic where 1 + 1 = 0. This is the same idea as the rank test in SP 800-22. Random grids nearly always have rank within a few of full.

    Example 6.4: Binary rank 31x31

    If 40,000 grids produced counts 220, 5130, 23050, 11600 for ranks at most 28, then 29, 30, 31, then against the expected counts below, \(\chi^2 = 0.678\). With 3 degrees of freedom, that gives \(p = 0.878\).

    The mathematics.

    \[p_r = 2^{r(2M-r)-M^2} \prod_{i=0}^{r-1} \frac{(1-2^{i-M})^2}{1-2^{i-r}}, \qquad \chi^2 = \sum_{\text{classes}} \frac{(\mathrm{obs} - \mathrm{exp})^2}{\mathrm{exp}}\]
    • \(M = 31\), and each row is the leftmost 31 bits of one 32-bit integer.
    • With 40,000 grids, the expected counts are 211.4 for rank at most 28, 5134.0 for rank 29, 23103.0 for rank 30 and 11551.5 for rank 31. The formula reproduces all four.

    What it catches and misses. It catches linear dependence between successive words in the upper bits. Bit 32 of each integer is never used here. Also, generators with a linear recurrence longer than the grid can look fine.

    6.5 Binary rank 32x32¶

    The idea in plain words. This is the same experiment with a slightly larger grid, and this time each row is a whole 32-bit integer. Any bit position that is stuck or tied to other bits will start to pull ranks down. Think of a jury in which some members just copy others.

    Example 6.5: Binary rank 32x32

    Counts of 230, 5100, 23200, 11470 for ranks at most 29, then 30, 31, 32 give \(\chi^2 = 2.844\) and, on 3 degrees of freedom, \(p = 0.416\). If a generator could never reach full rank, the count for rank 32 would be 0, and that term alone would add 11551.5 to \(\chi^2\).

    The mathematics.

    \[\chi^2 = \sum_{\text{classes}} \frac{(\mathrm{obs} - \mathrm{exp})^2}{\mathrm{exp}}, \qquad p = P\!\left(\chi^2_3 \ge \chi^2\right)\]
    • \(\chi^2_3\) is a chi-square variable with 3 degrees of freedom, from 4 classes.
    • The expected counts for 40,000 grids are 211.4 for rank at most 29, 5134.0 for rank 30, 23103.0 for rank 31 and 11551.5 for rank 32.
    • The rank formula is the same as for the 31x31 test, with \(M = 32\).

    What it catches and misses. It catches dependence across all 32 bits, including poor low-order bits. As with its smaller sibling, 1024 bits per grid is far too few to expose a long recurrence.

    6.6 Binary rank 6x8¶

    The idea in plain words. Now the grid is small and not square: pick one byte from each of six integers and stack them into a 6 by 8 grid. Because only one byte of each integer is used, this test can look at each byte position in turn, like inspecting each lane of a road separately.

    Example 6.6: Binary rank 6x8

    Against 100,000 grids, counts of 950, 21650, 77400 for ranks at most 4, then 5, 6 give \(\chi^2 = 0.541\). On 2 degrees of freedom, that gives \(p = 0.763\).

    The mathematics.

    \[p_r = 2^{r(R+C-r)-RC} \prod_{i=0}^{r-1} \frac{(1-2^{i-R})(1-2^{i-C})}{1-2^{i-r}}, \qquad \chi^2 = \sum_{r \in \{\le 4,\, 5,\, 6\}} \frac{(\mathrm{obs}_r - \mathrm{exp}_r)^2}{\mathrm{exp}_r}\]
    • \(R = 6\) rows and \(C = 8\) columns.
    • For 100,000 grids, the expected counts are 944.3, 21743.9 and 77311.8.
    • The chosen byte moves through 25 windows, bits 1 to 8 through 25 to 32, and a KS test combines the 25 p-values.

    What it catches and misses. It catches a weak byte position, which a test on whole words might dilute. It looks at only 48 bits at a time, so it is sensitive only to very local linear structure.

    6.7 Bitstream¶

    The idea in plain words. Picture a monkey typing on a two-key keyboard and write out every overlapping 20-bit word. There are \(2^{20}\) possible words, and we produce \(2^{21}\) of them, so on average each is typed twice. Yet by pure chance, some are never typed. The number of missing words is the test.

    Example 6.7: Bitstream

    Each word is missing with probability about \(e^{-2}\), so the expected number missing is \(2^{20} e^{-2} = 141{,}909.3\), in line with the published 141,909. A run with 141,000 missing words gives \(z = (141000 - 141909)/428 = -2.12\).

    The mathematics.

    \[z = \frac{j - 141909}{428}, \qquad p = \Phi(z)\]
    • \(j\) is the number of the \(2^{20}\) possible 20-bit words that never appear.
    • The stream holds \(2^{21} + 19\) bits, giving \(2^{21}\) overlapping words.
    • The 428 is specific to the overlapping version. Dieharder's notes give 290 for independent samples.
    • The test is repeated 20 times, giving 20 p-values.

    What it catches and misses. It catches any bias towards some 20-bit patterns, without needing to know which ones. It is not sensitive to dependence beyond 20 bits apart.

    6.8 OPSO¶

    The idea in plain words. OPSO stands for overlapping pairs sparse occupancy. Our monkey now has a keyboard of 1024 letters, each made from 10 chosen bits of a 32-bit integer, and it types overlapping two-letter words. We count the words it never typed. It gives a sharp view of how pairs of numbers fit together.

    Example 6.8: OPSO

    With 2 letters of 10 bits, there are \(2^{20}\) possible words and \(2^{21}\) are typed, so the expected number missing is again 141,909. A count of 142,000 gives \(z = (142000 - 141909)/290 = 0.314\) and \(p = 0.623\).

    The mathematics.

    \[z = \frac{j - 141909}{290}, \qquad p = \Phi(z)\]
    • \(j\) is the number of missing two-letter words, from \(2^{21} + 1\) keystrokes.
    • The 10-bit window moves through 23 positions, bits 1 to 10 through 23 to 32.
    • Dieharder's notes describe the \(\sigma = 290\) as the true value to three significant figures, rather than a simulation result. The dieharder code uses 290.4622634038.

    What it catches and misses. It catches dependence between consecutive numbers in specific bit fields. It looks only at adjacent pairs and only at the chosen 10 bits at a time.

    6.9 OQSO¶

    The idea in plain words. OQSO stands for overlapping quadruples sparse occupancy. The keyboard now has just 32 letters, from 5 chosen bits per integer, and the words are four letters long. It still counts missing words, but now four numbers in a row must cooperate.

    Example 6.9: OQSO

    Again \(32^4 = 2^{20}\) words and \(2^{21}\) typed. A count of 142,000 gives \(z = (142000 - 141909)/295 = 0.308\).

    The mathematics.

    \[z = \frac{j - 141909}{295}, \qquad p = \Phi(z)\]
    • \(j\) is the number of missing four-letter words, from \(2^{21} + 3\) keystrokes.
    • The mean comes from theory, and \(\sigma = 295\) from extensive simulation.
    • The 5-bit window moves through 28 positions.

    What it catches and misses. It catches dependence across four consecutive numbers in small bit fields. Like its siblings, it reports only an overall count, so it does not say which word was odd.

    6.10 DNA¶

    The idea in plain words. The keyboard shrinks to four letters, C, G, A and T, from two chosen bits per integer, and the words are ten letters long. So each word stretches across ten numbers in a row. This is the longest memory of the monkey tests.

    Example 6.10: DNA

    There are \(4^{10} = 2^{20}\) words and \(2^{21}\) are typed. A count of 142,000 gives \(z = (142000 - 141909)/339 = 0.268\).

    The mathematics.

    \[z = \frac{j - 141909}{339}, \qquad p = \Phi(z)\]
    • \(j\) is the number of missing ten-letter words, from \(2^{21} + 9\) keystrokes.
    • \(\sigma = 339\) comes from simulation.
    • The 2-bit window moves through 31 positions.

    What it catches and misses. It catches weak individual bit pairs and dependence over ten successive outputs. It reads only 2 bits of each number, so it says little about the rest.

    6.11 Count-the-1s on a stream¶

    The idea in plain words. Count the 1 bits in each byte and turn the count into a letter. Middling counts are common, extreme ones are rare, so the five letters have known odds. Then ask whether five-letter words made this way turn up as often as they should. It is a test of how neighbouring bytes relate through their weight alone.

    Example 6.11: Count-the-1s on a stream

    The bytes 0x31, 0xF0, 0x07, 0xAA, 0xFF have 3, 4, 3, 4, 8 ones, so they spell BCBCE. That word has probability \((56 \cdot 70 \cdot 56 \cdot 70 \cdot 37)/256^5 = 0.000517\), so it should appear about 132 times in 256,000 words. A statistic of \(Q_5 - Q_4 = 2600\) gives \(z = 1.41\).

    The mathematics.

    \[Q_k = \sum_{\text{words}} \frac{(\mathrm{obs} - \mathrm{exp})^2}{\mathrm{exp}}, \qquad z = \frac{(Q_5 - Q_4) - 2500}{\sqrt{5000}}\]
    • Counts of 0, 1, 2 ones become A; 3 becomes B; 4 becomes C; 5 becomes D; and 6, 7, 8 become E.
    • The letter probabilities are 37, 56, 70, 56 and 37 out of 256.
    • \(Q_5\) and \(Q_4\) cover 5-letter and 4-letter overlapping words. Subtracting handles the overlap.
    • The degrees of freedom are \(5^5 - 5^4 = 2500\). The Diehard text writes this as \(5^4 - 5^3\), which would be 500, so we take that as a slip.

    What it catches and misses. It catches bytes whose weights depend on their neighbours, and generators with poor bit balance. Different bytes with equal weight look identical to it.

    6.12 Count-the-1s on specific bytes¶

    The idea in plain words. This is the same game with one change. Rather than using every byte, we take one specified byte from each integer. That lets us aim the test at one position at a time, like testing each finger of a typist separately.

    Example 6.12: Count-the-1s on specific bytes

    With a window of bits 9 to 16, the integer 0x12F0A507 contributes byte 0xF0, which has 4 ones and so becomes the letter C. A statistic of 2600 gives \(z = 1.41\) and \(p = 0.921\), the same scale as the stream test.

    The mathematics.

    \[z = \frac{(Q_5 - Q_4) - 2500}{\sqrt{5000}}, \qquad p = \Phi(z)\]
    • \(Q_5\) and \(Q_4\) are as defined for the stream test.
    • Words come from 256,000 integers, one chosen byte each.
    • The byte window moves through 25 positions, bits 1 to 8 through 25 to 32.

    What it catches and misses. It catches defects tied to one byte position, which a whole-stream count might wash out. A defect shared evenly across all bytes is more likely to be picked up by the stream test.

    6.13 Parking lot¶

    The idea in plain words. Try to park cars at random in a 100 by 100 lot. If a new car would hit one already parked, it goes away and another tries. After 12,000 attempts, a fairly stable number of cars has parked. Clumpy or lattice-like generators leave too many or too few spots.

    Example 6.13: Parking lot

    If 3500 cars parked, \(z = (3500 - 3523)/21.9 = -1.05\) and \(p = 0.147\).

    The mathematics.

    \[z = \frac{k - 3523}{21.9}, \qquad p = \Phi(z)\]
    • \(k\) is the number of cars parked after 12,000 attempts.
    • There is no exact theory, so the mean and \(\sigma\) come from simulation.
    • A KS test combines 10 such values.

    The Diehard text speaks of circles of radius 1, while dieharder's notes describe square cars. In a quick simulation of ours, a crash rule where both coordinates differ by at most 1 averages about 3520, which fits the constant. Circles of radius 1 average near 1400, which does not.

    What it catches and misses. It catches poor uniformity of successive pairs in the plane. It is a fairly gentle test, sensitive mostly to gross two-dimensional structure.

    6.14 Minimum distance¶

    The idea in plain words. Scatter 8000 points in a big square and find the two closest. That smallest gap follows a known pattern. Points that are too neat give gaps that are too big, and clumped points give gaps too small. Think of the closest pair of strangers in a crowded square.

    Example 6.14: Minimum distance

    A closest pair with \(d^2 = 0.5\) gives \(1 - e^{-0.5/0.995} = 0.395\), a perfectly ordinary value.

    The mathematics.

    \[p = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\frac{d^2}{0.995}\right)\]
    • \(d\) is the smallest distance among the \((n^2 - n)/2\) pairs of \(n = 8000\) points in a square of side 10,000.
    • \(d^2\) is approximately exponential with mean 0.995, so \(p\) should be uniform.
    • This is repeated 100 times, with a KS test on the 100 values.

    Dieharder's notes say this expression is not accurate enough for very large numbers of trials, and call its own version with corrections a replacement.

    What it catches and misses. It catches pairs of outputs that land too close or too far apart in the plane. With only 100 repetitions, it can detect only large errors in the density of points.

    6.15 3D spheres¶

    The idea in plain words. This is the closest-pair idea in three dimensions. Scatter 4000 points in a cube, grow a sphere around each until it touches its nearest neighbour, and take the smallest sphere. Triples of outputs that fall on planes will show up here.

    Example 6.15: 3D spheres

    A smallest radius with \(r^3 = 10\) gives \(1 - e^{-10/30} = 0.283\). The mean volume is \((4/3)\pi \cdot 30 = 125.66\), matching the text's \(120\pi/3\).

    The mathematics.

    \[p = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\frac{r^3}{30}\right)\]
    • \(r\) is the smallest nearest-neighbour distance among 4000 points in a cube of edge 1000.
    • \(r^3\) is approximately exponential with mean 30, a value found by extensive simulation.
    • Twenty such samples go into a KS test.

    What it catches and misses. It catches poor three-dimensional uniformity, such as the plane structure of weak linear congruential generators. It uses only 20 values in the KS test, so its power against subtle defects is limited.

    6.16 Squeeze¶

    The idea in plain words. Start with a huge number and keep multiplying it by a random fraction, rounding up, until you reach 1. Count the steps. Under good randomness, the number of steps follows a known pattern, with a typical squeeze taking a couple of dozen steps. Bad generators reach 1 too fast or too slowly.

    Example 6.16: Squeeze

    Starting from 100 for simplicity, the fractions 0.5, 0.7, 0.3, 0.9, 0.2, 0.4 give 50, 35, 11, 10, 2, 1, so \(j = 6\).

    The mathematics.

    \[k_0 = 2^{31}, \qquad k_{t+1} = \lceil k_t U_{t+1} \rceil, \qquad j = \min\{t : k_t = 1\}\]
    \[\chi^2 = \sum_{\text{cells}} \frac{(\mathrm{obs} - \mathrm{exp})^2}{\mathrm{exp}}, \qquad \text{with 42 degrees of freedom}\]
    • \(U_t\) are uniforms made from the file's integers.
    • This is repeated 100,000 times, with cells for \(j \le 6\), each \(j\) from 7 to 47, and \(j \ge 48\).

    What it catches and misses. It catches defects that affect products of successive uniforms and the upper bits. It uses each number only through its size, so it is not sensitive to the low-order bits.

    6.17 Overlapping sums¶

    The idea in plain words. Add 100 uniforms, then slide along by one and add again. Each sum should be close to bell-shaped, and neighbouring sums share almost everything. In principle, you can untangle them into independent normals and test those. In practice, this one has a known problem.

    Example 6.17: Overlapping sums

    With a window of 3 and values 0.2, 0.9, 0.4, 0.7, the sums are 1.5 and 2.0, sharing two of their three terms. At the real window of 100, each sum has mean 50 and variance \(100/12 = 8.33\), and neighbouring sums have covariance \(99/12 = 8.25\).

    The mathematics.

    \[S_i = \sum_{j=0}^{99} U_{i+j}, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(S_i, S_{i'}) = \frac{100 - \lvert i - i' \rvert}{12} \text{ when } \lvert i - i' \rvert < 100\]
    • \(U_i\) are uniforms in \([0, 1)\).
    • A linear transformation is meant to give independent standard normals, which become uniforms for a KS test.
    • Ten KS p-values then go into one final KS test.

    What it catches and misses. It is meant to catch slow drift in the mean. Robert Brown's dieharder documentation rates it "Do Not Use" and says it gives a non-flat p-value distribution even for gold-standard generators. His lagged-sums test covers the same ground more reliably, so we would not rely on this one.

    6.18 Runs up and down¶

    The idea in plain words. A run up is a stretch of values that keep rising, and a run down is a stretch that keeps falling. In random data, long runs are rare in a precisely known way. It is like counting how many days in a row a share price keeps going up.

    Example 6.18: Runs up and down

    Marsaglia's own example, 0.123, 0.357, 0.789, 0.425, 0.224, 0.416, 0.95, starts with an up-run of length 3, then a down-run of length 2, then an up-run of at least 2.

    The mathematics.

    \[Q = (c - \mathrm{E}[c])^{T} \Sigma^{+} (c - \mathrm{E}[c])\]
    • \(c\) holds the counts of runs of each length, for runs up or for runs down, and \(\mathrm{E}[c]\) their expected values.
    • \(\Sigma^{+}\) is the weak inverse of their covariance matrix, and \(Q\) is compared with a chi-square distribution.
    • Each sequence has 10,000 numbers. It is run ten times, then in three more sets of ten.

    TestU01's guide gives 6 degrees of freedom for the closely related statistic it uses, with run lengths of 6 or more merged.

    What it catches and misses. It catches short-term trends and correlated order between successive values. It uses order only, not magnitude, so a generator with sound ordering but poor values can pass.

    6.19 Craps¶

    The idea in plain words. Play the dice game craps 200,000 times using the generator. We know exactly how often you should win and how long games should last. A casino would notice odd dice very quickly, and this is the same check. Unusually, it is two tests in one: the win rate and the length of games.

    Example 6.19: Craps

    The win probability is exactly \(244/495 = 0.492929\), so 200,000 games should give about 98,585.9 wins with \(\sigma = 223.6\). If you see 98,800 wins, \(z = 0.958\) and \(p = 0.831\). The chance of a game ending on the first throw is \(1/3\).

    The mathematics.

    \[z = \frac{w - 200000p}{\sqrt{200000\,p(1-p)}}, \qquad p = \frac{244}{495}\]
    \[\pi_1 = \frac{1}{3}, \qquad \pi_{i+1} = \frac{27(27/36)^{i-1} + 40(13/18)^{i-1} + 55(25/36)^{i-1}}{648} \text{ for } i = 1, \ldots, 19\]
    • \(w\) is the number of wins.
    • \(\pi_s\) is the probability that a game ends on throw \(s\), and all games of 21 throws or more share a final cell.
    • A chi-square on those 21 cells, with 20 degrees of freedom, tests game length. We checked these formulas against an exact count.

    Each throw of one die is \(1 + \lfloor 6U \rfloor\), using one 32-bit integer.

    What it catches and misses. It catches gross dependence between dice throws and weak upper bits. Only the top few bits of each integer decide a die's value, so anything lurking in the lower bits goes unseen.

    6.20 About Dieharder¶

    Robert G. Brown of Duke University wrote dieharder, with Dirk Eddelbuettel and David Bauer credited as authors of some versions, as an open, extensible successor to Diehard. It makes four main changes. It draws from a generator directly, so it can use as much data as you like instead of a fixed file. If you do feed it a file and the file runs short, it rewinds, which can distort results, so piping a stream in is better. It uses 100 p-values for its final KS test by default, far more than most of Diehard. It rates its own tests: most are Good, OPERM5 is Suspect and overlapping sums is Do Not Use. And it adds tests: its own RGB tests below, some from NIST's suite (monobit, runs and serial), and others such as Marsaglia and Tsang's GCD test. Where the original tests overlap samples, dieharder prefers non-overlapping versions.

    Reading the output is simple in principle. The manual sets the null hypothesis as a perfect generator for any seed, and flags a p-value as weak below 0.5 percent or above 99.5 percent. Those two tails total 1 percent, so that is about one test in 100 on good data. The manual says "at the 1% level total", yet also says one test in two hundred. For failed, the option defaults give one in a million, although one passage of the manual says 0.05 percent, and we could not reconcile those two. Brown is honest that the assessment labels deserve suspicion: a good generator will probably show weak on 0, 1 or 2 tests in a full run, and may fail one test in about 10 such runs. Options exist to resolve ambiguity by adding samples until a weak result clears or fails, or to test a generator to destruction.

    6.21 RGB bit distribution (Dieharder)¶

    The idea in plain words. Take words of \(n\) bits from the stream, without overlap, in small handfuls of 64. For any one word value, the number of times it turns up in a handful should follow a binomial pattern. It is like checking not only that all faces of a die come up equally often, but that their counts in each batch vary by the right amount.

    Example 6.21: RGB bit distribution (Dieharder)

    For \(n = 2\), each 2-bit value has chance \(1/4\) per draw. So the chance that a value appears exactly 16 times out of 64 is 0.1145. Over 1000 handfuls, you would expect about 114.5 such handfuls.

    The mathematics.

    \[E_b = t\binom{64}{b} p^b (1-p)^{64-b}, \qquad p = 2^{-n}, \qquad \chi^2 = \sum_{b} \frac{(x_b - E_b)^2}{E_b}\]
    • \(n\) is the tuple size and \(t\) is the number of handfuls (tsamples, default 100,000).
    • \(x_b\) is how many handfuls contain a given value exactly \(b\) times.
    • Bins with small expected counts are pooled.
    • Each run computes a p-value per value and keeps one chosen at random, and a KS test combines 100 of these (psamples).

    For \(n\) above 12, Brown says to raise tsamples so that each bin averages about 30 occurrences.

    What it catches and misses. It catches bias and clumping in \(n\)-bit words, and by raising \(n\) you can see where a generator starts to fail. Because each run keeps only one p-value, a defect in a few word values may take many runs to show. The source comments admit that a proper combination is still missing.

    6.22 RGB generalised minimum distance (Dieharder)¶

    The idea in plain words. Marsaglia's closest-pair idea is extended here to 2, 3, 4 or 5 dimensions, with a better formula. Scatter points in a unit cube and find the closest pair. Brown's version includes correction terms from M. Fischler, so that the formula stays accurate when you run many more trials.

    Example 6.22: RGB generalised minimum distance (Dieharder)

    Take \(d = 2\), \(n = 1000\) and \(r = 0.001\). Then \(V = \pi r^2 = 3.1416 \times 10^{-6}\), the exponent is \(-1.5692\), and the correction factor is 1.00397. So \(p = 0.79097\), against 0.79179 with no correction.

    The mathematics.

    \[p = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\frac{n(n-1)V}{2}\right)\left(1 + \frac{2 + Q_d}{6}\,n^3 V^2\right)\]
    \[V = \frac{\pi^{d/2} r^d}{(d/2)!} \ (d \text{ even}), \qquad V = \frac{2(2\pi)^{(d-1)/2} r^d}{d!!} \ (d \text{ odd})\]
    • \(n\) is the number of points, \(d\) the dimension, and \(r\) the smallest distance.
    • \(Q_d\) is 0.4135, 0.5312, 0.6202 and 1.3789 for \(d = 2\) to 5.
    • The trial count sets how small a density error can be seen. Brown's comments say Marsaglia's \(n = 8000\) would need about 2500 trials to detect a steady 20 percent error in two dimensions.

    What it catches and misses. It catches clumping or excess regularity of points in up to five dimensions, at a scale you can set through \(n\). Without wraparound, edges matter, which is what the correction terms address. It sees only the closest pair, so it does not describe the rest of the distribution.

    6.23 RGB permutations (Dieharder)¶

    The idea in plain words. Take \(n\) numbers at a time and note their order. All \(n!\) orderings should be equally likely. Unlike OPERM5, the groups do not overlap, so the counts are independent and an ordinary chi-square works. Simple often beats clever, and this is the simple version.

    Example 6.23: RGB permutations (Dieharder)

    For \(n = 3\) there are 6 orderings. Over 600 groups you expect 100 each. Counts of 110, 95, 100, 90, 105, 100 give \(\chi^2 = 2.5\) and, on 5 degrees of freedom, \(p = 0.776\).

    The mathematics.

    \[\chi^2 = \sum_{\text{orderings}} \frac{(\mathrm{obs} - t/n!)^2}{t/n!}, \qquad \text{with } n! - 1 \text{ degrees of freedom}\]
    • \(n\) is the group size.
    • \(t\) is the number of groups (tsamples, default 100,000).
    • A KS test combines the p-values from 100 runs (psamples).

    What it catches and misses. It catches ordering preferences, with no worry over overlap correlations. It uses only relative order and looks only within each group, so a dependence that spans group boundaries may go unseen. It also needs more data than an overlapping test to reach the same sensitivity.

    6.24 RGB lagged sums (Dieharder)¶

    The idea in plain words. Add up a long string of uniforms, skipping a fixed number of values between each one you use. The total should sit near half the count. If the generator has a hidden link at that lag, the total drifts. Brown offers this as the reliable replacement for overlapping sums.

    Example 6.24: RGB lagged sums (Dieharder)

    With \(t = 1{,}000{,}000\) terms, the mean is 500,000 and \(\sigma = \sqrt{1000000/12} = 288.68\). A total of 500,500 gives \(z = 1.73\) and a two-sided \(p = 0.083\).

    The mathematics.

    \[z = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{t} U_{i(\ell+1)} - t/2}{\sqrt{t/12}}\]
    • \(U\) are uniforms, \(\ell\) is the lag (the values skipped between terms), and \(t\) is the number of terms (tsamples, default 1,000,000).
    • The sum is approximately normal, and it becomes a p-value through the error function.
    • A KS test combines the p-values from 100 runs (psamples).

    What it catches and misses. It catches slow drift and correlation at a chosen lag. Since it adds up values, it responds mainly to the upper bits, and any defect that keeps the mean right will pass. You also have to scan through the lags yourself, as a defect at an untested lag stays hidden.

    6.25 Summary¶

    • Diehard reads about 10 to 12 million bytes as 32-bit integers. Most tests return a p-value that should be uniform on \([0,1)\), and many combine repeated runs with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.
    • Hundreds of p-values come out, so a value below 0.025 or above 0.975 is not a failure by itself. A stream that truly fails gives p-values of 0 or 1 to six or more places.
    • The families of tests are: lattice and spacing (birthday spacings, minimum distance, 3D spheres), occupancy of sparse words (bitstream, OPSO, OQSO, DNA), linear dependence (binary ranks), order and size (OPERM5, runs, squeeze, overlapping sums, craps) and geometry (parking lot).
    • Dieharder draws from a generator directly, uses 100 p-values for its final KS test, and labels tests Good, Suspect or Do Not Use. Overlapping sums is rated Do Not Use.
    • Every test reports only an overall result, so a failure says that something is wrong but rarely what.

    6.26 Exercises¶

    1. In the birthday spacings test, 8 birthdays fall in a 64-day year. Compute \(\lambda=n^3/(4m)\). What does it represent?

      Answer

      \(\lambda=8^3/(4\times64)=512/256=2\). It is the expected number \(j\) of values that occur more than once among the sorted gaps between birthdays; under randomness that count is approximately Poisson with mean \(\lambda\).

    2. Craps is played 10,000 times with the generator under test. The exact win probability is \(244/495\). Find the expected number of wins and its standard deviation.

      Answer

      Expected wins: \(10{,}000\times244/495=4929.3\). The standard deviation is \(\sqrt{10{,}000\times(244/495)\times(251/495)}=50.0\). A count more than about 150 away (three standard deviations) would be suspicious.

    3. A Diehard run prints a p-value of 0.97 on one test and 0.04 on another, and nothing else is unusual. Should you reject the generator?

      Answer

      No. Marsaglia's advice is that with hundreds of p-values a value below 0.025 or above 0.975 is not a failure by itself, and the normal approximations behind many of them are weakest in the tails. A genuinely failing stream gives p-values of 0 or 1 to six or more places.

    4. List the four main changes that Dieharder made to Diehard.

      Answer

      It draws from a generator directly, so it can use as much data as wanted instead of a fixed file; it uses 100 p-values for its final KS test by default; it rates its own tests (Good, Suspect, Do Not Use); and it adds tests, including its own RGB tests, some from the NIST suite, and others. Where Diehard overlaps samples, it prefers non-overlapping versions.

    5. Why is the OPERM5 or overlapping-sums test treated with caution in Dieharder, and what does that teach about writing a test?

      Answer

      Dieharder rates OPERM5 as Suspect and overlapping sums as Do Not Use, because they give non-flat p-value distributions even for good generators. A test is valid only if it fails good data at the rate it claims, so a test must be calibrated against known-good data before its failures are believed.
