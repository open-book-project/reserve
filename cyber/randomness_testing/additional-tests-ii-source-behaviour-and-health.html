    Chapter 11: Additional Tests (II): Source Behaviour and Health¶

    Physical sources misbehave in ways that algorithms do not: they drift as temperature or voltage change, their histograms are smooth because the analog signal is continuous, and they can stall. The three tests of this chapter are aimed at raw data from a physical source. Stationarity and runs up and down look for drift, the analog fingerprint looks for a smooth histogram, and the SP 800-90B health tests act as smoke alarms. A conditioner erases most of these signs, so these tests are mainly for raw data.

    11.1 Stationarity and runs up and down (test 11)¶

    The idea in plain words. Watch a runner across 64 legs of a race: is she slowing down? Physical sources drift as temperature or voltage changes, so we check whether the average byte and the share of ones stay put along the file. Then we look at a stock chart and count its streaks of ups and downs. Trending or sticky data has too few streaks, and jittery data has too many.

    Example 11.1: Stationarity and runs up and down

    For the values 3, 7, 5, 6, 8, 2, the moves are up, down, up, up, down, which makes \(R = 4\) runs. For \(n = 6\), the expected number is \((2 \times 6 - 1)/3 = 3.667\), with variance \((96 - 29)/90 = 0.744\). So \(z = 0.386\) and \(p = 0.699\). For the drift part, take 4 blocks of 1000 bits holding 470, 490, 510 and 530 ones. The overall share is 0.5, each block's variance is 250, and \(\chi^2 = (900 + 100 + 100 + 900)/250 = 8\). With 3 degrees of freedom, \(p = 0.046\).

    The mathematics. The first part is one-way ANOVA (analysis of variance, which asks whether group averages differ by more than the scatter inside the groups) of byte values over 64 blocks, and the second is a chi-square test of the share of ones across 64 blocks:

    \[ F = \frac{\sum_b n_b (\bar{x}_b - \bar{x})^2 / 63}{\sum_b \sum_i (x_{b,i} - \bar{x}_b)^2 / (n - 64)} \sim F(63,\ n-64) \]
    \[ \chi^2 = \sum_{b=1}^{64} \frac{(S_b - t_b \hat{q})^2}{t_b \hat{q} (1 - \hat{q})} \sim \chi^2(63) \]

    The third counts runs up and down in \(u_i = x_i + U_i\), on up to \(2^{20}\) bytes:

    \[ E[R] = \frac{2n-1}{3}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}[R] = \frac{16n-29}{90}, \qquad p_{\text{runs}} = \mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{|R - E[R]|}{\sqrt{2\,\mathrm{Var}[R]}}\right) \]
    • \(n\): the number of bytes used, and \(\bar{x}\) their mean.
    • \(x_{b,i}\), \(\bar{x}_b\), \(n_b\): the bytes of block \(b\), their mean and their count.
    • \(S_b\), \(t_b\): the ones and total bits in block \(b\).
    • \(\hat{q}\): the share of ones over the whole sample.
    • \(U_i\): independent uniform noise between 0 and 1.
    • \(R\): the number of runs in the \(u_i\) series.

    The final value is \(p = \min(1,\ 3\min(p_{\text{mean}}, p_{\text{bits}}, p_{\text{runs}}))\). The block means are averages of about 16,000 values at 1 MiB, so the normal assumption behind ANOVA is sound.

    What it catches and misses. False alarms: 1 of 120. It flagged 10 of 59: 8 raw physical, RANDU on one seed only (\(p = 8.5 \times 10^{-5}\), while the other seed gave 0.17) and parity. Failing this test also feeds the analog condition in the verdict. It misses drift that is short compared with a block, and a conditioner will erase drift completely, so it mainly tests raw data.

    We found and fixed a defect here too. The formulas for \(R\) hold for continuous data, but bytes tie about 1 time in 256. Our first version dropped ties, which shifted the expected run count by about 0.2%. That sounds tiny, but it is about 3 standard deviations at \(n = 2^{20}\), and it failed 52% of good samples. Adding \(U_i\) fixes this cleanly: if the bytes are i.i.d., then \(x_i + U_i\) is exactly continuous i.i.d., so the formulas hold exactly.

    11.2 Analog fingerprint (test 12)¶

    The idea in plain words. Draw a bar for each byte value and look at the skyline. Random data gives a jagged comb, because each bar's height has nothing to do with its neighbours'. An analog sensor read through a converter gives rolling hills, because value 100 and value 101 are nearly the same voltage. So we ask one narrow question: do neighbouring bars have similar heights?

    Example 11.2: Analog fingerprint

    A toy with 8 bars. The smooth hill 2, 5, 9, 12, 12, 9, 5, 2 has neighbour correlation \(r = 0.611\). For this toy, \(z = r\sqrt{6} = 1.50\) and \(p = 0.067\). The same bars scrambled as 12, 2, 9, 5, 12, 2, 5, 9 give \(r = -0.703\), \(z = -1.72\) and \(p = 0.958\). Eight bars are too few for a firm answer. At the full 256 bars, \(r = 0.05\) gives \(p = 0.21\), \(r = 0.2\) gives \(p = 0.00072\), and \(r = 0.4\) gives \(p = 9.2 \times 10^{-11}\).

    The mathematics. Count each byte value, then correlate the counts of neighbouring values:

    \[ r = \mathrm{corr}\bigl((c_0, \dots, c_{254}),\ (c_1, \dots, c_{255})\bigr), \qquad z = r\sqrt{254} \approx N(0, 1) \]
    \[ p = \tfrac{1}{2}\, \mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \]
    • \(c_v\): the number of times byte value \(v\) occurs.
    • \(r\): the Pearson correlation over the 255 neighbouring pairs.
    • \(z\): its standardised value. Under the uniform multinomial null, the counts are exchangeable (reordering them does not change their joint law) with only a tiny negative correlation.

    The p-value is one-sided, because only "smoother than chance" counts as a fingerprint. If all counts are identical, the code sets \(r = 1\).

    What it catches and misses. False alarms: 0 of 120, with p-values that look uniform. It flagged 11 of 59 (10 at the verdict line): 8 raw physical, 2 weak generators (MINSTD on both seeds) and one os.urandom sample at \(p = 0.0048\), which is simply the expected occasional chance result. It missed the simulated drifting source, since drift does not shape a histogram. A spiky or lopsided histogram without smoothness passes too: RANDU fails test 1 at \(p = 0\), yet got \(p = 0.51\) here. And MINSTD shows that a smooth histogram is not proof of hardware. On its own this test cannot change a verdict; it only explains other failures as analog.

    11.3 SP 800-90B health tests (test 13)¶

    The idea in plain words. These are the two smoke alarms that NIST approves for real entropy sources. A developer may replace them with their own tests that meet the standard. One goes off if the same value repeats too many times in a row, as if the source got stuck. The other goes off if one value turns up too often in a short window. Both are set by how much entropy the source claims, so that a healthy source trips them only about once per million samples.

    Example 11.3: SP 800-90B health tests

    If \(H = 2\) bits per sample, the repetition cutoff is \(C = 1 + \lceil 20/2 \rceil = 11\) and the proportion cutoff is 177 out of 512. Those match the standard's examples, and our code reproduces its whole table: 410, 311, 177, 62 and 13 for \(H\) of 0.5, 1, 2, 4 and 8.

    Now typical good data. The most common byte appears about 4275 times in 1 MiB. Then \(p_u = 0.00424\) and \(H = 7.88\), so the cutoffs are 4 repeats and 13 of 512. A run of 4 equal bytes is expected only \(2^{20} \times 2^{-24} = 0.0625\) times. If we see 1 such alarm, its p-value is 0.632, which means nothing. If we see 10, it is \(1.1 \times 10^{-7}\).

    The mathematics. With \(\alpha = 2^{-20}\) per sample, and \(H\) from the most-common-value upper bound as in test 1:

    \[ C_{\text{RCT}} = 1 + \left\lceil \frac{20}{H} \right\rceil, \qquad C_{\text{APT}} = 1 + \mathrm{CRITBINOM}(512,\ 2^{-H},\ 1 - 2^{-20}) \]
    \[ A_{\text{RCT}} \le_{\text{st}} \mathrm{Binomial}(N, \alpha), \qquad A_{\text{APT}} \le_{\text{st}} \mathrm{Binomial}(W, \alpha) \]
    \[ p = \min\bigl(1,\ 2\min(p_{\text{RCT}}, p_{\text{APT}})\bigr) \]
    • \(H\): the assessed min-entropy per byte.
    • \(C_{\text{RCT}}\): the run length that raises a repetition count alarm.
    • \(C_{\text{APT}}\): the count in a 512-byte window (of that window's first value) that raises an adaptive proportion alarm.
    • CRITBINOM: the smallest count whose binomial cumulative probability reaches the third argument.
    • \(A_{\text{RCT}}, A_{\text{APT}}\): the alarm counts. The symbol \(\le_{\text{st}}\) means "no larger than, in distribution".
    • \(N\), \(W\): the number of bytes and of non-overlapping 512-byte windows.
    • \(p_{\text{RCT}}, p_{\text{APT}}\): the upper tail probabilities of those binomials at the observed alarm counts, or 1 if there were no alarms.

    \(H\) comes from the same sample's upper bound. If \(H\) is so low that no window could reach the cutoff, as on constant data, the source has no usable entropy and the test returns \(p = 0\). At 1 MiB the true APT window rate is slightly above nominal, though the Bonferroni factor 2 keeps \(p\) valid at 0.01 and at the verdict line.

    What it catches and misses. False alarms: 0 of 120 (every good sample gets \(p = 1\)). It flagged 4 of 59: only the real CPU jitter samples (raw bytes and low bit, on both measurements). That is by design. These alarms are for a source that stalls or sticks, not for mild imperfection, and the other raw physical samples did not trip them. Note one difference from a real deployment. There, \(H\) comes from the design claim, while here it is measured from the sample itself, so a source that is evenly bad raises its own cutoffs. Treat this as a check on the sample, not as a replacement for health tests running in the device.

    11.4 Summary¶

    • Stationarity combines one-way ANOVA of block means, a chi-square on the share of ones across 64 blocks, and runs up and down on \(x_i+U_i\) with \(U_i\) uniform noise, which removes the effect of ties exactly.
    • Ignoring ties in the runs formula shifted the expected run count by about 0.2%, about 3 standard deviations at \(n=2^{20}\), and failed 52% of good samples. Adding \(U_i\) fixes it.
    • The analog fingerprint correlates the counts of neighbouring byte values: a smooth histogram gives a positive \(r\). It is one-sided and cannot change a verdict on its own.
    • The health tests (RCT and APT) are set by the claimed entropy so that a healthy source trips them about once per million samples; they catch a source that stalls, not mild imperfection.

    11.5 Exercises¶

    1. For the values 3, 7, 5, 6, 8, 2, count the runs up and down and give the expected number and variance of \(R\) for \(n=6\).

      Answer

      The moves are up, down, up, up, down, so \(R=4\) runs. \(E[R]=(2n-1)/3=11/3=3.667\) and \(\mathrm{Var}[R]=(16n-29)/90=67/90=0.744\). Then \(z=(4-3.667)/\sqrt{0.744}=0.386\) and \(p=0.699\).

    2. Four blocks of 1000 bits contain 480, 495, 505 and 520 ones. Test whether the share of ones is stable across blocks.

      Answer

      The overall share is \(\hat q=0.5\) and each block has variance \(1000\times0.25=250\). So \(\chi^2=(400+25+25+400)/250=3.4\) on 3 degrees of freedom, giving \(p=0.334\). There is no sign of drift.

    3. Why must the runs-up-and-down test add independent uniform noise \(U_i\) to the bytes?

      Answer

      The formulas for the number of runs assume continuous data, but bytes tie about 1 time in 256. Dropping ties shifted the expected run count by about 0.2%, which is about 3 standard deviations at \(n=2^{20}\) and failed 52% of good samples. With \(U_i\) uniform on \((0,1)\) and independent, \(x_i+U_i\) is exactly continuous i.i.d. when the bytes are i.i.d., so the formulas hold exactly.

    4. Find the repetition count cutoff for a source assessed at \(H=2\) bits per sample and say when an alarm is raised.

      Answer

      \(C_{RCT}=1+\lceil20/2\rceil=11\). An alarm is raised when a sample repeats 11 or more times in a row.

    5. A smooth histogram of byte counts is observed. Does it prove the data came from hardware? Use the example of MINSTD.

      Answer

      No. The analog fingerprint detects only "smoother than chance". MINSTD, a pseudo-random generator, also produced a smooth histogram, so smoothness is evidence about the data, not proof of a physical origin. That is also why the test cannot change a verdict on its own.
