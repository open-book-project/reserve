    Chapter 10: Choosing RAM and Comparing the Families¶

    The earlier chapters described each family on its own. This chapter sets them side by side. It compares the families on the properties engineers care about, gives a guide to the best memory for common products as of 2026, adds buying and design advice, and ends with the bandwidth arithmetic of every family so that the comparison is backed by numbers. Read the use-case tables as snapshots: they say what was the best choice in mid-2026, a cheaper or older alternative, and what is likely to replace it next.

    10.1 Family comparison¶

    Table 10.1 compares the families. The ratings are relative to each other, not absolute.

    Family Bandwidth Latency Power Capacity Cost per GB Best for
    SRAM Very high (on-chip) Lowest (~1 ns) Medium (leakage) Tiny (MBs) Highest Caches
    HBM Highest (TB/s) Medium Low per bit Medium (GBs per stack) Very high AI and HPC
    GDDR Very high Higher High Medium High Graphics
    LPDDR High Medium Lowest Medium Medium Mobile, laptops
    DDR Medium Medium Medium Highest (TBs per server) Lowest PCs, servers
    NV-RAM (MRAM etc.) Low to medium Medium Low (zero at rest) Small Very high Persistent embedded storage

    Table 10.1: Family comparison.

    The takeaway

    SRAM is fastest, HBM has the most bandwidth, LPDDR uses the least power, DDR gives the most capacity per dollar, and GDDR balances bandwidth against cost for graphics.

    Each family is therefore a point in the space of speed, power, capacity and cost. No family wins every column, which is why a single system often contains several: SRAM caches, DRAM main memory, and perhaps HBM, flash and non-volatile memory.

    10.2 Best type by use case¶

    The tables below summarise the best memory choice for common products in 2026, a cheaper or older alternative and what is likely to come next.

    Phones, laptops and desktops

    Use case Best choice now (2026) Why it wins Budget / alternative Coming next
    Flagship phones LPDDR5X (8533–10,700 MT/s) Lowest power per bit; stacked on or right next to the SoC LPDDR5 LPDDR6 (first flagships 2026)
    Budget phones LPDDR4X Cheap and power-efficient enough LPDDR4 LPDDR5 moving down-market
    Thin and light laptops LPDDR5X, soldered High bandwidth for the integrated GPU and long battery life LPDDR5 LPDDR6
    Upgradeable or gaming laptops DDR5 SO-DIMM (5600–6400) or LPCAMM2 Can be upgraded; LPCAMM2 gives LPDDR speed in a replaceable module DDR4 SO-DIMM (older platforms) LPCAMM2 / CSODIMM replacing SO-DIMM
    Gaming desktops DDR5-6000 CL30 (AMD); DDR5-6400 to 8000+ CUDIMM (Intel) Sweet spot of speed versus latency DDR5-5600 DDR6 (~2027+)
    Apple Silicon Macs LPDDR5X unified memory On-package, very wide bus LPDDR5 LPDDR6

    Servers, accelerators and graphics

    Use case Best choice now (2026) Why it wins Budget / alternative Coming next

    General servers / cloud DDR5 RDIMM (6400) with ECC Reliability (RAS) features; terabytes per socket DDR4 RDIMM (older servers) DDR6 RDIMM
    Bandwidth-heavy servers (HPC) MRDIMM (8800 MT/s) About 40 per cent more bandwidth than standard RDIMMs DDR5 RDIMM MRDIMM Gen 2 (12,800)

    10.3 Design and buying guidance¶

    Soldered or upgradeable? Soldered LPDDR (or memory in the processor package) gives the best bandwidth and battery life because the wires are short and the interface is tuned for low power, but the capacity you buy is the capacity you keep for the life of the device. Socketed DDR5 or a CAMM-style module costs a little power and board space and lets you add or replace memory later. For a long-lived laptop or workstation, upgradeability, or simply buying more capacity up front, is usually worth more than a small speed gain.

    Use ECC when data integrity matters. On-die ECC in DDR5 and LPDDR5 protects only inside the chip. For servers, workstations, scientific computing, financial or medical data and long-running jobs, choose platforms with side-band ECC (RDIMM or ECC UDIMM) so that errors anywhere on the path are corrected and logged.

    Match the family to the bottleneck. For designers: bandwidth points to HBM or GDDR, power to LPDDR, capacity and cost to DDR, and latency to SRAM. Check long-term supply too, which matters most in automotive and industrial products.

    10.4 Bandwidth worked examples¶

    All of these use the formula of Section 1.6: bandwidth $= $ data rate \(\times\) bus width \(/ 8\). With per-pin rates in Gbps, the answer comes out directly in GB/s.

    Memory Calculation Peak bandwidth
    DDR5-6400 DIMM 6400 MT/s × 64 bits ÷ 8 ÷ 1000 51.2 GB/s
    LPDDR5X-9600, 64-bit bus 9600 MT/s × 64 bits ÷ 8 ÷ 1000 76.8 GB/s
    One GDDR7 chip (x32) at 32 Gbps 32 Gbps × 32 bits ÷ 8 128 GB/s
    HBM3E stack at 9.6 Gbps 9.6 Gbps × 1024 bits ÷ 8 ≈ 1229 GB/s ≈ 1.2 TB/s
    HBM4 stack at 8 Gbps 8 Gbps × 2048 bits ÷ 8 2048 GB/s ≈ 2 TB/s

    To get a whole system's bandwidth, multiply by the number of channels, chips or stacks: a dual-channel DDR5-6400 desktop has about 102 GB/s, and an accelerator with eight HBM3E stacks about 8 to 10 TB/s. These are peak figures; sustained bandwidth is lower.

    Example 10.1: Pick the memory for a bandwidth target

    A designer needs 500 GB/s of peak memory bandwidth for a graphics processor. Options: GDDR6 at 16 Gbps, GDDR7 at 28 Gbps.

    1. Per chip (32 data pins): GDDR6 at 16 Gbps gives \(16 \times 32 / 8 = 64\) GB/s; GDDR7 at 28 Gbps gives \(28 \times 32 / 8 = 112\) GB/s.
    2. Chips needed: GDDR6 \(500/64 = 7.8\), so 8 chips and a 256-bit bus (512 GB/s); GDDR7 \(500/112 = 4.5\), so 5 chips and a 160-bit bus, which give \(5 imes 112 = 560\) GB/s. (Four chips would give only 448 GB/s.)
    3. The faster memory needs fewer chips and a narrower bus, which simplifies the board, at the price of costlier memory and more careful signalling (Chapter 12).

    For comparison, one DDR5-6400 DIMM would give 51.2 GB/s, so ten DIMMs would be needed, far more than a graphics card can fit.

    10.5 Summary¶

    • No family wins every property: SRAM is fastest, HBM has the most bandwidth, LPDDR the lowest power, DDR the most capacity per dollar and GDDR the best bandwidth for graphics at a reasonable cost.
    • The best choice depends on the product: LPDDR for phones and thin laptops, DDR5 for desktops and servers, HBM for AI accelerators, GDDR for graphics, SRAM for caches, MRAM or FeRAM for radiation or persistence.
    • Soldered memory is faster and more efficient; socketed memory can be upgraded. Use side-band ECC when integrity matters.
    • Peak bandwidth is data rate times width divided by 8; multiply by channels or stacks for a system.

    10.6 Exercises¶

    Exercise 10.1. Pick a memory for each product and give the reason: (a) a flagship phone; (b) a cloud server; (c) a GPU for AI training; (d) an IoT sensor node.

    Answer

    (a) LPDDR5X, with the lowest power per bit and stacked next to the SoC. (b) DDR5 RDIMM with ECC, for reliability and terabytes per socket. (c) HBM3E (or HBM4), because the model's weights must be streamed at TB/s. (d) On-chip SRAM, possibly with external PSRAM: simple and very low power.

    Exercise 10.2. Compute the peak bandwidth of an LPDDR5X-10700 system with a 128-bit bus and compare it with a dual-channel DDR5-6400 desktop.

    Answer

    \(10{,}700 \times 128 / 8 / 1000 = 171.2\) GB/s against 102.4 GB/s: about 1.67 times as much.

    Exercise 10.3. A GPU uses a 384-bit bus of GDDR7 chips at 28 Gbps. How many chips and what bandwidth?

    Answer

    \(384/32 = 12\) chips. Bandwidth \(= 28 \times 384 / 8 = 1344\) GB/s.

    Exercise 10.4. Why is the sustained bandwidth of a DDR5 system lower than its peak? List three causes.

    Answer

    Refresh makes the bank busy, bank conflicts delay accesses to a row that needs another row closed, and switching between reads and writes costs idle time. Real programs typically reach 70 to 90 per cent of the peak.

    Exercise 10.5. An engineer proposes to use only on-die ECC to protect a server's memory. Explain why side-band ECC is still needed.

    Answer

    On-die ECC is computed inside each chip and fixes single-bit errors in the array, but it does not protect the bus or the wires on the board. Side-band ECC is computed by the memory controller, so it protects the whole path, and errors can be logged. Servers need that coverage.
