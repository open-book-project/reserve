    Preface¶

    About this book¶

    A modern digital chip begins as a few thousand lines of Verilog and ends as a layout file, many gigabytes in size, from which a factory makes photographic masks. Everything between those two files is the subject of this book: logic synthesis, floorplanning, power planning, placement, clock-tree synthesis, routing, parasitic extraction, static timing analysis, physical verification and the final stream-out to GDSII. Together these steps are called the RTL-to-GDSII flow.

    The book is a course text. It explains each stage in continuous prose, works the numbers by hand (slack, die size, wirelength, clock skew, wire delay), shows the real commands and configuration of the open-source flow, and then asks you to run that flow yourself on a small design. Nothing in it needs an industrial licence or a non-disclosure agreement. The tools are the free ones: Yosys for synthesis, OpenROAD for floorplanning, placement, clock-tree synthesis, routing and timing, Magic and KLayout for layout and design-rule checks, netgen for layout-versus-schematic checks, all driven by OpenLANE and targeting the SkyWater Sky130 open process kit.

    In brief

    Read the chapters in order. Each one opens with what it covers and why it matters, builds its ideas in numbered sections, shows worked examples in boxes, and closes with a summary and exercises whose answers are folded away until you open them.

    Who the book is for¶

    The book assumes that you know what a Verilog module is and have written a little synthesisable RTL: registers, an always block, an adder. It does not assume any knowledge of physical design. It suits a final-year undergraduate or a postgraduate course on VLSI design or digital design automation, and a self-taught reader who wants to understand what happens after the simulation passes. A reader who has met the companion books on hardware description languages and on microarchitecture will find the flow here a natural next step: those books describe the chip, this one makes it.

    How the chapters are arranged¶

    Chapter Subject The question it answers
    1 The RTL-to-GDSII flow What are the stages, files, goals and tools?
    2 Logic synthesis and timing basics How do gates come out of RTL, and are they fast enough?
    3 Floorplanning and power planning How big is the chip, and how does power reach every cell?
    4 Placement Where does every cell sit?
    5 Clock-tree synthesis Does the clock reach every flip-flop together?
    6 Routing and parasitic extraction Can every net be drawn in metal, and what do the wires cost?
    7 Static timing analysis With real wires, is every path fast enough and not too fast?
    8 Physical verification and tapeout Can the factory build it, and is it the right circuit?
    9 Running the flow with OpenLANE How do I do all of this myself, for free?

    Timing ideas are introduced early, in Chapter 2, because synthesis is where timing first matters, and they are completed in Chapter 7 once clock skew and real wire delays can be included. The exercises that need a computer with the tools installed are collected at the end of Chapter 9 together with a capstone project; the paper exercises sit at the end of the chapter whose ideas they test.

    How to use the book¶

    Work the paper problems before opening the answers. Slack, utilisation, wirelength and skew are all short calculations, and the habit of doing them by hand is what lets you read a tool's report with understanding. When you reach Chapter 9, keep a log of every command, configuration change and result; the tool exercises ask for tables built from each run's metrics file, a screenshot of the layout and a note of what changed between runs.

    A word on numbers

    Figures in worked examples are teaching values, chosen so that the arithmetic is easy to follow. Where a number comes from a real process, such as the Sky130 site size or a design-rule minimum, the text says so. Rule values given in the book are simplified for teaching and must never be used in place of the foundry's own rule manual.

    Conventions¶

    Names of files, commands, configuration variables and cells are set in code font. Times are in nanoseconds (ns) or picoseconds (ps), lengths in micrometres (µm) and millimetres (mm), areas in µm². A figure or table is numbered by its chapter and its place in the chapter, as in Figure 3.1. Exercises are in boxes; each answer is in a folded box directly below it.
